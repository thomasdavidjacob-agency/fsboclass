Legal Disclosures by State

Complete Course30 min readModule 9 of 12

Seller disclosure is one of the most legally consequential parts of selling a home — and one of the most misunderstood. Failing to disclose a known material defect can expose you to lawsuits years after closing. But disclosure doesn't mean you have to confess every scratch and squeaky floorboard. It means providing the information buyers need to make an informed decision about a property whose condition only you — as the current owner — truly know. This module explains exactly what you're required to disclose, what you're not, how AS-IS sales work, and where to find your state's specific forms.

Disclosure Protects Your Proceeds

Failure to disclose is one of the most common post-sale legal claims against FSBO sellers — and post-sale litigation can erase the commission savings you worked hard to keep. This module keeps you legally protected so your equity stays yours.

What Is Seller Disclosure?

Seller disclosure is the legal obligation to inform buyers of material facts about a property that could affect its value or a buyer's decision to purchase. A "material fact" is anything a reasonable buyer would consider important — not necessarily every minor imperfection, but certainly anything that would meaningfully affect the property's value, safety, or the buyer's enjoyment of it.

Disclosure requirements vary significantly by state. Some states (California, for example) have comprehensive 11-page disclosure forms covering dozens of specific items. Others have minimal formal requirements and rely more on the general common-law principle that sellers must disclose known material defects. Understanding what your state requires is essential — and non-compliance isn't excused by ignorance.

The Universal Federal Disclosure: Lead Paint

Regardless of your state's specific requirements, one disclosure is federally mandated for virtually all residential sales: the EPA Lead-Based Paint Disclosure.

If your home was built before 1978, federal law (the Residential Lead-Based Paint Hazard Reduction Act) requires you to:

  • Provide buyers with the EPA pamphlet "Protect Your Family from Lead in Your Home"
  • Disclose any known lead-based paint hazards in the home
  • Provide any available records or reports related to lead paint (prior inspections, remediation records)
  • Give buyers a 10-day window to conduct a lead paint inspection or risk assessment before the contract becomes binding (buyers can waive this right in writing)

This disclosure applies to all sellers — FSBO and agent-represented alike — and failure to comply can result in fines up to $10,000 per violation plus potential civil liability. The disclosure form is available for free from the EPA website.

Lead Paint Disclosure Is Non-Negotiable

This is federal law with real enforcement teeth. If your home was built before 1978, complete the Lead-Based Paint Disclosure form before accepting any offer. It takes five minutes and protects you from significant legal exposure.

What You Must Disclose: The General Standard

Across virtually all states, the core disclosure obligation is the same even where specific forms differ: you must disclose any known material defect that would not be readily discoverable by a buyer through reasonable inspection.

Examples of Items That Must Be Disclosed

  • Known roof leaks or prior water intrusion, even if repaired
  • Foundation problems, settling, or structural issues
  • History of flooding, drainage problems, or basement water intrusion
  • Mold — current presence or known prior remediation
  • Pest infestations (termites, rodents) — current or prior
  • Known HVAC, electrical, or plumbing deficiencies
  • Unpermitted additions or improvements
  • Deaths that occurred in the home (requirements vary by state — some require disclosure, many do not)
  • Known environmental hazards: underground storage tanks, proximity to contaminated sites
  • Neighborhood nuisances that affect the property: noise, odors, zoning changes in progress
  • Homeowner Association disputes or pending special assessments
  • Boundary disputes or easements that affect use of the property

The "You Know It" Standard

A critical nuance: disclosure applies to what you know, not what a perfect investigation would uncover. You're not required to hire specialists to discover issues you have no reason to suspect. But if you know your basement floods every spring, you cannot simply not mention it because you fixed the immediate damage. The history of the defect — even if repaired — is generally material information.

What You Are NOT Required to Disclose

Many sellers over-disclose out of anxiety, which can create problems rather than prevent them. Here's what the law generally does not require you to disclose:

  • Conditions you genuinely didn't know about — you can only disclose what you know
  • Minor cosmetic defects that are visible and obvious
  • In most states: whether the home was the site of a crime, suicide, or natural death (check your specific state — some require disclosure of murders)
  • Whether the home is considered "haunted" or has a psychological stigma (most states explicitly exempt this)
  • Information about neighbors (fair housing rules limit what sellers can discuss about neighborhood demographics)
  • Defects you had professionally repaired with documented evidence — though the history of the defect itself may still need disclosure

State-by-State Disclosure Overview

While state-specific forms change and you should always consult your state's current requirements, here is a general framework by region:

High-Disclosure States (Comprehensive Forms Required)

California: The California Transfer Disclosure Statement (TDS) is one of the most comprehensive in the country — sellers must complete a detailed multi-page form covering every known physical condition of the property. California also requires a Natural Hazard Disclosure (NHD) report, which must be purchased from a third-party provider.

Texas: The Texas Seller's Disclosure Notice covers dozens of specific items. Texas is a "let the buyer beware" state historically, but the disclosure form is comprehensive for what it does require.

New York: The NY Property Condition Disclosure Statement is required — though sellers may opt to provide a $500 credit to the buyer in lieu of completing it (most buyers and their attorneys insist on the form anyway).

Florida: Florida requires disclosure of all known material defects and has specific requirements around sinkholes, Chinese drywall, and flood zone status.

Moderate-Disclosure States

Oregon: Oregon's Seller Property Disclosure Statement covers property condition, environmental issues, title issues, and neighborhood conditions. FSBO sellers must complete it before accepting an offer.

Washington: The Washington Seller Disclosure Statement is required for most residential sales and covers physical condition, title, neighborhood, and environmental issues.

Colorado: Colorado uses the Seller's Property Disclosure form, which is extensive but largely based on actual knowledge rather than requiring investigation.

Limited-Disclosure States

Alabama, Arkansas, North Dakota, Wyoming: These states have minimal formal disclosure requirements and operate more under common-law principles. "Caveat emptor" (buyer beware) traditions are stronger here, but the duty to disclose known material defects still exists as a matter of law.

AS-IS Sales: What They Mean and What They Don't

An AS-IS sale does not eliminate your disclosure obligations. This is one of the most persistent misconceptions in real estate. Here's what AS-IS actually means:

What AS-IS Means

An AS-IS sale signals that you are not willing to make repairs or provide credits in response to inspection findings. You're selling the home in its current physical condition and are not agreeing upfront to fix anything. This is a negotiating position, not an exemption from disclosure law.

What AS-IS Does NOT Mean

AS-IS does not mean you can conceal known defects. You must still disclose everything you know — you're just not agreeing to address what's found. Buyers can still conduct inspections and exit the contract based on inspection findings (unless they've waived the inspection contingency). They're just put on notice that you won't negotiate repairs.

A seller who lists AS-IS and fails to disclose a known material defect is not protected by the AS-IS label. Courts have consistently held that AS-IS clauses do not excuse fraudulent concealment or intentional non-disclosure.

When AS-IS Makes Sense

AS-IS sales are common in estate sales, foreclosure-adjacent situations, or when a seller knows there are significant issues and has priced accordingly. If you're selling AS-IS at a below-market price that reflects the home's condition, it's a legitimate strategy — but you still must disclose what you know.

How to Protect Yourself Legally

1. Be Thorough and Honest

When in doubt, disclose. The cost of over-disclosing (a buyer negotiates a small credit) is far lower than the cost of non-disclosure litigation. Real estate lawsuits based on failed disclosure can drag on for years and cost tens of thousands in legal fees — far more than any repair or credit would have cost.

2. Document Everything

Keep records of all repairs, inspections, and maintenance work you've had done on the home. These records support your disclosure statements and demonstrate good faith. Receipts from contractors, inspection reports, warranty documents — keep them all and provide copies to the buyer at closing.

3. Get Your Disclosure Form in Order Early

Complete your seller disclosure form before you list — not after you're under contract. Review it carefully with fresh eyes, walk through the home with the form in hand, and answer each question truthfully based on your actual knowledge. Have a family member or trusted friend review it for completeness.

4. Consider a Pre-Listing Inspection

Hiring your own inspector before you list ($300–$500) gives you a complete picture of your home's condition and eliminates surprises during the buyer's inspection. Anything the pre-listing inspection finds either gets fixed proactively or gets disclosed. This approach reduces negotiation friction after you're under contract — one of the most common deal-killers in FSBO transactions.

Where to Find Your State's Disclosure Forms

Your state's residential seller disclosure form is typically available from one of these sources:

  • Your state's real estate regulatory commission website (search "[state] real estate commission disclosure form")
  • Your state's Association of Realtors website (forms are often publicly available)
  • Your title company or closing attorney — they'll have the current standard form
  • Your flat-fee MLS service — many include disclosure forms as part of their package

HOA Disclosure Requirements

If your home is in a homeowner's association, your disclosure obligations expand significantly — and the consequences of incomplete HOA disclosure are some of the most common sources of post-closing disputes in residential transactions. Buyers who discover an undisclosed special assessment, a contentious HOA dispute, or an HOA in financial distress after closing have legitimate legal claims against you, regardless of how thorough your property condition disclosures were.

What You Must Provide to HOA Buyers

The specific documents required vary by state, but the following represent what buyers and their attorneys will expect and what you should have ready before accepting an offer:

  • CC&Rs (Covenants, Conditions & Restrictions): The foundational governing document that defines what owners can and cannot do with their property. Contact your HOA management company for the current version — they change over time and buyers need the current version, not the one you received when you bought.
  • Bylaws and rules and regulations: The operational rules of the HOA — meetings, voting, enforcement procedures. These documents frequently contain restrictions that affect buyers (rental restrictions, pet policies, parking rules) that are material to their decision to purchase.
  • Current financial statements and reserve fund status: This is the most frequently overlooked HOA disclosure item and the one with the largest financial consequences. A healthy HOA maintains a reserve fund equal to at least 70% of its projected capital replacement needs. An HOA with a depleted reserve is a financial liability for buyers — it signals that future special assessments are likely.
  • Meeting minutes from the last 12–24 months: Buyers' attorneys and agents request meeting minutes specifically to identify disputes, complaints, ongoing legal actions, and discussions about upcoming expenses. Minutes that reveal a contentious lawsuit, a major repair project, or chronic maintenance issues are material to buyers.
  • Any pending or approved special assessments: A special assessment is a one-time charge levied against all owners to fund a capital expense the reserve fund cannot cover. If your HOA has approved or is actively discussing a special assessment — even if the final vote hasn't happened — this is material information buyers must have. Concealing a pending $5,000 special assessment is a textbook non-disclosure claim.
  • Your current monthly dues and any scheduled increases: Buyers factor monthly HOA dues into their mortgage qualification calculation. If dues are scheduled to increase, that affects their budget — it's material.
  • Any HOA violations issued to you and their current status: Open violations against your unit must be disclosed. Buyers inherit the property's compliance standing; an unresolved violation can prevent them from making improvements or can result in fines they didn't cause.

The HOA Review Period

In most states with significant HOA populations, there is a statutory HOA disclosure period — typically 5–10 business days — during which the buyer receives all HOA documents and has the right to exit the contract without penalty if they are dissatisfied with what they find. California's HOA review period is 3 days. Texas gives buyers 3 days after receiving the resale certificate. Florida allows buyers to terminate for any reason within 3 business days of receiving HOA documents. Plan for this review period in your closing timeline — it occurs after contract acceptance and before the inspection contingency expires, and failing to deliver HOA documents promptly delays the entire closing schedule.

HOA Financial Instability Is a Legitimate Buyer Concern

If your HOA has an underfunded reserve, a pending special assessment, or is involved in litigation, buyers are entitled to this information — and it will surface during the title company's HOA resale certificate process even if you don't disclose it proactively. Attempting to close before buyers discover HOA financial problems creates the worst possible outcome: a transaction that collapses at or near closing, potentially after you've already made commitments on your next home. Disclose HOA financial issues upfront, price your home with them in mind, and let buyers make an informed decision.

Flood Zone Status and FEMA Map Disclosures

Flood risk has become one of the most consequential disclosure issues in residential real estate over the past decade — and in many states, FSBO sellers face specific legal obligations around it that go beyond the general "disclose what you know" standard. Even in states that don't explicitly require flood zone disclosure, failure to disclose known flood risk that you're aware of creates significant post-closing liability.

How to Determine Your Property's Flood Zone Status

The definitive source for federal flood zone designations is FEMA's Flood Map Service Center at msc.fema.gov. Enter your property address to see the current FEMA flood map panel that covers your property. The flood zone designation matters enormously:

  • Zone X (unshaded): Minimal flood hazard. No federal flood insurance requirement. No specific disclosure obligation in most states, though any known personal flooding history should still be disclosed.
  • Zone X (shaded): Moderate flood hazard — 0.2% annual chance of flooding (500-year floodplain). Flood insurance not federally required but may be advisable. Disclose if you're aware the property is in this zone.
  • Zone A or Zone AE: High flood hazard — the 100-year floodplain. FEMA Special Flood Hazard Area (SFHA). If your buyer is using a federally backed mortgage, flood insurance is mandatory before the lender will fund. Flood insurance costs — which can run $1,500–$4,000 per year or more under FEMA's Risk Rating 2.0 pricing methodology — directly affect the buyer's ability to qualify for the loan and their monthly carrying cost. This is unambiguously material information.
  • Zone V or VE: Coastal high velocity flood hazard. The most severe designation, typically in oceanfront areas. Flood insurance is mandatory and expensive; building restrictions are significant.

State-Specific Flood Disclosure Requirements

Several states have enacted specific flood disclosure laws that impose requirements beyond the general material fact standard. Florida requires disclosure of known flood damage. Texas requires disclosure of whether the property has been damaged by flooding, whether it is in a 100-year floodplain, and whether it is in a flood pool area. Oregon requires disclosure of known flood zone status. Coastal states generally have the most stringent requirements. Even in states with minimal formal requirements, buyers routinely check FEMA maps themselves — a failure to disclose that a buyer discovers independently is far worse than a proactive disclosure.

FEMA Risk Rating 2.0 Has Changed the Calculation

FEMA's Risk Rating 2.0 methodology, implemented in 2021, fundamentally changed how flood insurance premiums are calculated. Previous pricing was based on simple flood zone maps; the new methodology uses property-specific risk factors including distance to water, elevation, first-floor height, and property value. The result: many properties that had artificially low flood insurance premiums now face significantly higher costs — and some properties outside traditional flood zones are being priced at elevated risk. If your property is near water, any water body, or in an area with significant rainfall events, buyers may ask about flood insurance costs proactively. Being able to provide a current flood insurance quote (or directing buyers to the FEMA Flood Map Service Center) is a sign of transparency that builds buyer confidence.

Dealing With Unpermitted Work

Unpermitted improvements are among the most common sources of post-closing litigation in residential real estate — and they're far more prevalent than most sellers realize. If you've owned a home for 10–15 years and completed any significant improvement — a basement finish, a garage conversion, an addition, a deck, a bathroom addition — there's a real chance at least some of that work was completed without a building permit, especially if you used informal contractors or did the work yourself.

Why Unpermitted Work Creates Problems for Buyers

Buyers face two specific financial risks from unpermitted improvements:

  1. Appraisal exclusion: Appraisers are required to note the permitted status of improvements. An unpermitted finished basement or garage conversion may not be counted in the home's square footage for appraisal purposes, directly reducing the appraised value. If your transaction price is partly justified by unpermitted square footage, the appraisal may come in short — and you'll face a price renegotiation after contract.
  2. Municipal enforcement risk: Building departments can and do require property owners to retroactively bring unpermitted work up to code or remove it entirely. The cost of retroactive permitting can range from nominal to catastrophic depending on what was built and what code requires. An unpermitted addition that would require demolition to bring to code is a material defect that affects the property's value by the full cost of remediation.

Your Options When You Have Known Unpermitted Work

  • Disclose and price accordingly: The cleanest approach. Note the unpermitted improvement on your disclosure form, indicate that permits were not obtained, and price the property reflecting the risk the buyer is assuming. Some buyers — particularly investors and cash buyers — will accept unpermitted work at a discount.
  • Retroactively permit before listing: Many municipalities have processes for retroactive permitting, sometimes called "after-the-fact" permits or permit amnesty programs. The process typically involves an inspection of the existing work by the building department, identification of any code compliance corrections required, completion of those corrections, and issuance of a final permit. This can take 30–120 days depending on your municipality and is worth pursuing if the improvement is significant and the permitting process is feasible.
  • List AS-IS and disclose: Disclose the unpermitted work explicitly, list AS-IS so buyers understand you won't be making corrections, and let buyers factor the risk into their offer. This is legally defensible and practically effective for sellers who cannot or choose not to retroactively permit.

The one path that is not available to you: failing to disclose known unpermitted work and hoping it doesn't surface. Home inspectors frequently note visible signs of unpermitted work — non-standard construction methods, mismatched ceiling heights, wiring or plumbing that doesn't meet code. Appraisers check permit records. Title companies often pull permit history as part of their title search. Concealment of known unpermitted work is one of the clearest bases for a post-closing fraud claim, and it's a claim courts take seriously.

The Pre-Listing Inspection Report: How to Present It

If you complete a pre-listing inspection — which this course recommends — you face an immediate strategic decision: do you share the report with buyers, and if so, how? There's no universally correct answer. Both the case for sharing and the case for strategic restraint have merit, and the right approach depends on your specific situation.

The Case for Sharing the Full Report

Proactively sharing your pre-listing inspection report establishes credibility as a transparent seller in a way that almost nothing else can. Buyers who receive a pre-listing report from the seller experience the negotiation differently — they're not waiting for the buyer's inspector to "discover" problems and then use them as leverage. They already have the information. In many transactions, sharing the pre-listing report results in fewer post-contract inspection surprises, faster inspection negotiations (because you've already addressed significant items), and buyers who are less likely to use the inspection as a price renegotiation tactic. The transparency often translates directly into a cleaner path to close.

The Case for Strategic Restraint

A 60-page pre-listing inspection report is not a curated marketing document. Inspectors flag every visible concern, including items that a buyer's inspector might note less severely, rate as lower priority, or miss entirely. Handing buyers a roadmap of every inspector notation in your home — including items that are truly minor, cosmetic, or normal for the age of the home — can create unnecessary negotiating pressure around issues that would never have been raised otherwise. There's a reason experienced listing agents typically don't share pre-listing reports verbatim.

The Middle-Ground Approach

Address and Summarize — Don't Hand Over the Raw Report

The most effective approach for most FSBO sellers: complete the pre-listing inspection, address all significant findings before going on the market (either by repairing them or by disclosing them on your seller disclosure form with a contractor quote attached), and then tell prospective buyers: "We completed a pre-listing inspection and addressed the significant findings. Our disclosure form reflects current conditions, and we're happy to share documentation of completed repairs upon request." This signals transparency and preparation without handing buyers a 60-page negotiating roadmap. If a buyer's agent presses for the full report, you can share it at your discretion — but you're not obligated to lead with it.

Key Takeaways

  • Disclosure applies to what you know — you're not required to investigate, but you cannot conceal what you're aware of
  • The federal Lead-Based Paint Disclosure is required for all homes built before 1978 — no exceptions
  • AS-IS sales do not exempt you from disclosure requirements — they only signal you won't make repairs after inspection
  • Disclosure requirements vary significantly by state — California, Texas, Florida, and New York have the most comprehensive forms
  • When in doubt, disclose — the cost of a repair credit is always lower than the cost of post-closing litigation
  • A pre-listing inspection eliminates surprises and reduces post-contract negotiation friction — worth the $300–$500 investment
  • HOA sellers must provide CC&Rs, bylaws, financial statements, meeting minutes, and disclosure of any pending special assessments — the HOA review period must be built into your closing timeline
  • Flood zone status (check msc.fema.gov) is material information for buyers whose lenders require flood insurance — FEMA's Risk Rating 2.0 has made flood insurance costs a more prominent transaction factor since 2021
  • Unpermitted work must be disclosed — concealment is one of the clearest bases for post-closing fraud claims
  • For pre-listing inspection reports: address significant findings, reflect them on your disclosure form, and summarize what you've done rather than handing over the raw report

Action Steps

  1. Download your state's current seller disclosure form and read it in full — note every question you're unsure how to answer.
  2. If your home was built before 1978, download and complete the EPA Lead-Based Paint Disclosure form from epa.gov before accepting any offer.
  3. Walk through your home with the disclosure form in hand and answer each question based on your actual knowledge — don't guess, don't minimize.
  4. Gather records of all repairs, inspections, and maintenance work done during your ownership — compile them in a folder to provide to the buyer at closing.
  5. Consider scheduling a pre-listing inspection with a licensed home inspector — the $300–$500 cost nearly always pays for itself by reducing post-contract negotiation.

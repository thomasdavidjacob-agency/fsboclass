MODULE 10

Handling Inspections & Appraisals

Complete Course30 min readModule 10 of 12

Inspections and appraisals are the two most common points where real estate transactions stall, fall apart, or result in significant renegotiation. As an FSBO seller, you'll navigate both without an agent buffering the process. That's not a disadvantage — it means you can respond directly, decisively, and with a full understanding of what the findings actually mean. This module gives you the frameworks to handle both with confidence.

Inspection Credits Come Out of Your Pocket

Every repair credit or price reduction a buyer negotiates after inspection comes directly out of your proceeds. This module teaches you how to evaluate inspection findings, distinguish legitimate concerns from negotiating tactics, and counter strategically.

Part 1: The Home Inspection

What Inspectors Actually Look For

A licensed home inspector is a generalist who evaluates the visible and accessible components of a residential property and identifies conditions that appear deficient, safety-related, or beyond normal wear. Inspectors typically examine:

  • Roof: Condition of shingles, flashing, gutters, and fascia. Evidence of leaks or improper installation.
  • Foundation and structure: Visible cracks, settlement, moisture intrusion, framing issues in accessible areas.
  • Electrical system: Panel condition, wiring type (aluminum wiring is flagged in older homes), GFCI outlets in required locations, grounding, safety hazards.
  • Plumbing: Water pressure, visible pipe conditions, drainage, water heater age and condition, signs of leaks.
  • HVAC: Heating and cooling system age, condition, and operation. Duct condition where accessible.
  • Insulation and ventilation: Attic insulation levels, ventilation adequacy, signs of moisture damage.
  • Windows and doors: Condition, operation, sealing, evidence of moisture between panes.
  • Exterior: Siding condition, grading (does water drain away from the foundation?), decks and porches.
  • Interior: Walls, ceilings, floors — visible evidence of water damage, staining, or structural issues.

Understanding Inspection Report Language

Inspection reports can be alarming to read if you don't understand how inspectors categorize findings. Most reports use language like:

  • "Safety concern" or "immediate action required": Genuine safety issues like exposed wiring, gas leaks, or significant structural problems. These are the items that require attention.
  • "Recommend repair/replacement": Items that are deficient and should be addressed, but are not immediate safety hazards. These are negotiating points.
  • "Monitor": Items worth watching but not immediately actionable. Often used for minor cracks, early-stage wear, or conditions that may develop into larger issues.
  • "Noted" or "For your information": Items the inspector is flagging for awareness that don't require action. Normal maintenance items often fall here.

A 60-item inspection report sounds alarming. But if 50 of those items are "monitor" and "noted" notations — caulk the window trim, clean the dryer vent, replace the furnace filter — that's a normal report on a maintained home. Focus your attention and negotiation energy on the safety concerns and significant repair recommendations, not the entire document.

The Pre-Listing Inspection Advantage

If you complete your own pre-listing inspection before going on the market, you already know what's in the report — and you've either fixed the issues or priced accordingly. When the buyer's inspection comes in, it has no surprises for you. This dramatically reduces post-inspection renegotiation anxiety and positions you as a transparent, prepared seller.

Responding to an Inspection Report

After the buyer submits their inspection response (requesting repairs, credits, or price reduction), you have three options for each requested item:

Option 1: Agree and Repair

You agree to complete specific repairs before closing. Use licensed contractors, get everything in writing, and provide receipts at closing. Never perform significant repairs yourself unless you're genuinely qualified — a buyer can request proof of contractor work, and amateur repairs can create liability.

Best for: Safety items, items that would fail a lender's property condition requirements, and repairs where you can control the cost by getting your own competitive bids.

Option 2: Offer a Credit

Instead of making repairs yourself, you offer a dollar amount at closing that the buyer can use for repairs after they take ownership. Credits are often preferable to repairs: you don't have to manage contractors on a tight timeline, the buyer gets to choose their own contractor, and the transaction moves forward without repair delays.

When offering a credit: use actual contractor quotes where possible — don't just accept the buyer's numbers. Get 1–2 bids for significant items and offer the lower estimate. "I've received quotes of $3,200–$3,800 for the roof repair. I'm offering a $3,200 closing credit for this item."

Option 3: Decline

You can decline to repair or credit any inspection item. This terminates the negotiation on that point and the buyer must decide whether to proceed as-is, exit the contract (if within their inspection contingency window), or come back with a revised request. Declining is not the end of the deal — it's a negotiating position.

Best for: Items you believe are normal wear, items already factored into your price, items where the buyer's request is disproportionate to the actual repair cost, and situations where you've already disclosed the item.

What NOT to Concede On

Buyers (and their agents) sometimes use inspection reports to renegotiate price on items that have nothing to do with genuine defects. Watch for:

  • Requests based on items you already disclosed — they knew about it when they made the offer
  • Cosmetic issues (paint, carpet, caulk lines) bundled into a repair request
  • Items that appear on inspection reports of virtually every home of your age (older panel brands, older-style windows, etc.)
  • Requests where the amount is significantly disproportionate to actual repair cost — always get your own quotes

Special Inspections

Beyond the general home inspection, buyers may request or lenders may require specialized inspections. Know what each involves:

  • Termite/WDO inspection: Inspects for wood-destroying organisms. Many lenders require this. If evidence of active infestation is found, treatment is typically required before closing.
  • Radon test: Measures radon gas levels. EPA action level is 4 pCi/L. Mitigation systems run $800–$2,500. Common requirement in high-radon areas.
  • Sewer scope: Camera inspection of the main sewer line. Increasingly requested in older homes. Issues found can be significant — lateral line replacement runs $3,000–$10,000.
  • Mold test: Air quality testing for mold spores. Required when visible mold is noted in the general inspection or disclosed by the seller.
  • Septic inspection: Required for homes on septic systems. Includes pump-out and system evaluation.

Part 2: The Appraisal

For financed purchases, the buyer's lender orders an independent appraisal after the inspection period. The appraiser's job is to provide the lender with a professional opinion of the property's market value — because the lender is lending against the property's value, not the agreed purchase price.

How the Appraiser Works

The appraiser will schedule an appointment (typically 30–60 minutes at the property) to measure the home, note its condition, document features, and take photos. They'll then complete a desk review comparing your home to 3–6 comparable sales and arrive at an opinion of value. This process takes 5–14 days from the appointment to the delivery of the report to the lender.

If the Appraisal Comes In At Value

Great — the lender will fund the loan at the agreed price and you proceed to closing. This is the most common outcome in a well-priced transaction.

If the Appraisal Comes In Short

An appraisal that values the home below the purchase price (called a "low appraisal" or "appraisal gap") creates a problem: the lender will only lend up to the appraised value. For example, if you're under contract at $420,000 but the home appraises at $400,000, the lender will only fund based on $400,000. The $20,000 gap must be resolved. Your options:

  • Reduce the purchase price to the appraised value — the most common resolution. You take $20,000 less than agreed.
  • Ask the buyer to pay the gap in cash — the buyer brings an additional $20,000 to closing above what the lender funds. Only works if the buyer has the cash and motivation.
  • Split the gap — negotiate a price somewhere between the appraised value and the original purchase price. Both parties absorb part of the shortfall.
  • Challenge the appraisal — submit a formal reconsideration of value (ROV) with comparable sales data that supports your price. This is worth attempting if you have solid comps the appraiser missed or used incorrectly.
  • Walk away — if no resolution is possible and the buyer had an appraisal contingency, they can exit the contract and recover their earnest money.

How to Avoid a Low Appraisal

  • Price your home accurately using solid comps — overpriced homes appraise short
  • Prepare a comp package for the appraiser: a one-page summary of 3–4 recent comparable sales that support your price, with addresses and sale prices. Appraisers are required to consider this information.
  • Be present during the appraisal to answer questions and highlight features the appraiser might not immediately notice (recent mechanical updates, permits pulled, custom features)
  • Ensure the home is clean and well-presented — condition affects the appraiser's qualitative assessment
Challenging an Appraisal

If you believe the appraisal contains errors — wrong square footage, a comparable sale that isn't actually comparable, an important recent sale that was excluded — you can request a Reconsideration of Value through the buyer's lender. You cannot contact the appraiser directly (this is prohibited). Provide documentation and comparable sales data to the buyer, who submits it to the lender for the ROV.

FHA and VA Loan Appraisal Requirements: What FSBO Sellers Must Know

FHA and VA loans are among the most common financing types used by first-time buyers and military personnel — two buyer populations that FSBO sellers frequently encounter. What most sellers don't know: FHA and VA appraisers function as both value appraisers and property condition inspectors. They are required to flag specific conditions that a conventional appraiser would simply note or ignore, and the lender cannot fund the loan until those flagged conditions are resolved. This means that accepting an FHA or VA offer on a home with certain conditions creates repair obligations that accepting a conventional offer would not.

FHA Minimum Property Requirements (MPRs)

An FHA appraiser will flag — and the loan will not close without resolution of — any of the following conditions:

  • Exposed or dangling electrical wiring
  • Peeling or deteriorating paint on any surface in a home built before 1978 (lead paint risk)
  • Windows that are painted, nailed, or otherwise unable to open and close
  • Active roof leaks or evidence of ongoing water intrusion
  • Non-functional heating system (the home must have a heat source capable of maintaining 50°F in all living spaces)
  • No safe, unobstructed access to all areas of the home (access panels blocked, crawl space inaccessible)
  • Evidence of infestation — active insect or rodent evidence
  • Missing handrails on stairs of more than three steps
  • Broken or missing windows allowing weather infiltration

VA Minimum Property Requirements

VA loans include all of the FHA requirements above, plus several additional conditions specific to VA program standards:

  • No evidence of active wood-destroying organisms (active termites trigger a mandatory WDO inspection and treatment requirement)
  • Functioning, code-compliant sewage disposal (the VA verifier will note if the septic system shows obvious signs of failure)
  • Safe, potable water supply (private well properties require water testing for VA loans)
  • No major defects in foundation, framing, or roof that affect structural soundness
  • Adequate drainage preventing water accumulation around the foundation

Common Deal-Killing Inspection Items and Exact Response Strategies

Not all inspection findings are created equal. Certain items appear repeatedly in inspection reports and are specifically known to kill transactions — not because they're always genuinely catastrophic, but because buyers and their agents treat them as categorical deal-breakers without distinguishing between minor and serious presentations of the same issue. Knowing these items in advance — and having a response strategy ready — prevents panic and unnecessary concessions.

Roof at or Beyond Useful Life

An inspector who notes "roof is at or near the end of its useful life" is not saying the roof is currently leaking — they're estimating remaining service life. The appropriate response: obtain quotes from two licensed roofing contractors. If the roof is genuinely in need of replacement, quotes will typically run $7,000–$18,000 depending on home size, material, and pitch. Offer a closing credit at the lower of your two bids — not the buyer's estimate, not the inspector's assumption. If the roof has years of remaining life and the inspector's note is precautionary, document that with a contractor opinion letter and decline the credit.

Zinsco or Federal Pacific Electrical Panel

These specific panel brands (manufactured in the 1950s–1980s) are flagged by inspectors as potential fire hazards due to known manufacturing defects in their circuit breakers. Buyers, lenders, and homeowner insurance companies treat these findings seriously. Response strategy: get a licensed electrician to evaluate the specific panel. In many cases, panels can be replaced for $2,500–$4,000. Offer a credit at the lower of two electrician bids. Do not minimize this item — it is a legitimate safety concern that creates insurance complications for buyers. Addressing it cleanly keeps the deal moving.

Active Foundation Movement

An inspector who notes "active foundation movement" or "potential structural concern" is flagging something that requires a licensed structural engineer to evaluate — not a general contractor, not a foundation repair company with a sales incentive, and not your handyman. Get a structural engineer evaluation ($500–$800) before responding to any foundation-related request. The engineer's report will either confirm that concerns are minor (horizontal cracking from normal settling, not active movement) or identify genuine structural issues. Never respond to a foundation request with a credit without an engineer's opinion — you may be offering far more or far less than the actual issue warrants.

Polybutylene Plumbing

Polybutylene pipe was used in homes built roughly 1978–1995 and is no longer approved for new construction due to documented failure rates when exposed to oxidants in municipal water supplies. Inspectors flag it. Insurance companies sometimes refuse or surcharge coverage. Response strategy: obtain full-home repiping quotes from two licensed plumbers. Depending on home size, full repiping runs $2,500–$15,000. Offer a credit at the lower bid. Do not offer the higher quote as a starting point — let the buyer choose their own contractor and manage the cost difference.

Galvanized Steel Water Lines

Common in homes built before 1960, galvanized steel water lines corrode from the inside out over time, reducing water pressure and eventually failing. Inspectors flag them uniformly in older homes. The response is identical to polybutylene: two plumber quotes, offer credit at the lower. Note that galvanized steel line replacement is sometimes done in sections (kitchen, primary bath) rather than all at once — a phased quote may be significantly lower than full repiping.

Respond at the Right Risk Tier to Protect Your Proceeds

The most expensive inspection negotiation mistake is treating all inspection items as if they carry equal weight — and then conceding on mid- and low-tier items because you're already feeling pressured by the presence of a high-tier item. When a buyer's inspection request includes a roof concern, two minor electrical items, and cosmetic paint, the correct response addresses the roof strategically, declines the electrical items as normal for the age of the home, and declines the paint outright. Sellers who respond to the full list — out of anxiety about keeping the deal together — routinely give away $5,000–$12,000 that they didn't have to. Categorize first, respond second.

Challenging a Low Appraisal: Step-by-Step

A low appraisal feels like a verdict — an official document saying your home is worth less than you agreed to sell it for. It is not a verdict. It is one appraiser's opinion on one day, based on the comparable sales they selected and the adjustments they chose to make. Appraisals can be wrong, and you have a formal process available to challenge them. Use it before conceding on price.

Step 1: Obtain and Review the Full Appraisal Report

Buyers receive a copy of the appraisal report from their lender; sellers do not receive it automatically. Ask your buyer (or buyer's agent) for a copy. Review it carefully for three types of errors that form the basis for a successful Reconsideration of Value:

  • Factual errors: Wrong square footage (check against your permit records or assessor data), incorrect bedroom/bathroom count, missed improvements (the appraiser didn't note the finished basement), or wrong lot size.
  • Inappropriate comparable sales: A comp from a neighborhood that isn't truly comparable (different school district, across a major road, different market segment), a comp with dramatically different condition, or a distressed sale (foreclosure, estate sale) being used as a market-rate comp.
  • Missing comparable sales: Recent sales in your neighborhood that support your price but weren't included. Appraisers use the closest available comps; they may not know about an off-market or recently closed sale that you're aware of.

Step 2: Build Your Reconsideration of Value Package

A Reconsideration of Value (ROV) is a formal submission to the appraiser (through the lender) presenting specific, documented grounds for reconsidering their opinion of value. Your package should include:

  • A cover letter identifying each specific objection and the supporting documentation
  • For factual errors: documentation (assessor records, permit certificates, MLS records)
  • For each proposed comparable sale: the full sale record including address, sale date, sale price, square footage, bedroom/bathroom count, and a brief explanation of why this sale is more appropriate than what the appraiser used
  • A summary statement: "Based on the corrections and comparable sales identified above, we respectfully request reconsideration of the appraised value for [address]."

Step 3: Submit Through the Buyer's Lender

You cannot contact the appraiser directly — this is prohibited under federal appraisal independence rules. Provide your ROV package to the buyer, who submits it to their lender, who forwards it to the appraiser. The appraiser is required to consider and respond to the ROV but is not required to change their opinion. Strong ROVs with specific factual errors or genuinely superior comparable sales have meaningful success rates. ROVs based on general disagreement ("we think it should be worth more") rarely succeed.

Comparable Sale Criteria That Strengthen an ROV

To be persuasive, your proposed comparable sales should meet as many of these criteria as possible: sold within the last 90 days (not more than 6 months ago), located within 1 mile of your property (not just the same ZIP code), same bedroom and bathroom count, similar square footage (within 15%), similar age and construction, and similar condition (not a distressed sale or significant renovation). A comp that meets all six criteria and supports your price is a compelling ROV exhibit. One that meets only two or three is weak.

Managing the Timeline Between Contract and Close

As the FSBO seller, you are the de facto transaction coordinator for your own deal. There is no listing agent calling parties to check on status, no transaction coordinator sending reminder emails, and no one else watching the calendar on your behalf. If a milestone is missed — an earnest money deadline passes uncollected, a contingency period expires without resolution, a closing date is missed without a signed extension — the legal consequences fall on you. The solution is a transaction timeline you build at contract acceptance and actively manage through to close.

Key Milestone Categories

Every transaction has the same major categories of milestones, though exact timing varies by contract:

  • Earnest money deposit: Typically due 2–3 business days after contract execution. Confirm deposit with the title company by the deadline — if it hasn't arrived, contact the buyer's agent immediately. An earnest money deadline that passes without deposit is a contract default.
  • Inspection window: The buyer's window to complete their inspection and submit any inspection response. Track the expiration date. If the buyer's inspection contingency expires without a response, the buyer has typically waived their right to exit based on inspection — this is legally significant.
  • Financing contingency: The deadline by which the buyer must secure loan approval. If they cannot obtain financing by this date and have not waived or extended the contingency, they may exit the contract and recover their earnest money.
  • Appraisal completion: Typically completed within 20–25 days of contract. Follow up with the buyer's agent if you haven't heard appraisal results by day 25.
  • Final walkthrough: Typically 1–2 days before closing. The buyer inspects to verify the home's condition is unchanged from contract and that agreed repairs are complete.
  • Closing day preparation: Confirm your wire instructions with the title company at least 48 hours before closing. Confirm all agreed repairs are complete and receipts are ready. Confirm your moving timeline.

Key Takeaways

  • A 60-item inspection report is normal — focus only on safety concerns and significant repair recommendations, not the full list
  • For each inspection request, you have three options: repair, credit, or decline — credits are often preferable to repairs for timeline and control
  • Always get your own contractor quotes before offering credits — never simply accept the buyer's cost estimates
  • A low appraisal creates an appraisal gap with four resolution options: price reduction, buyer pays gap, split the gap, or challenge the appraisal
  • Prepare a comp package for the appraiser — a one-page summary of supporting comps you provide helps ensure they have the full picture
  • A pre-listing inspection eliminates inspection surprises and positions you as a transparent, prepared seller
  • FHA and VA appraisers flag specific property conditions (peeling paint, exposed wiring, non-functional HVAC) that will prevent loan funding — address these before the appraisal appointment
  • Common deal-killers (aged roof, legacy electrical panels, polybutylene plumbing) have established response strategies: two contractor quotes, credit at the lower bid
  • A low appraisal can be challenged through a formal Reconsideration of Value — provide factual corrections or superior comparable sales through the buyer's lender
  • Build a transaction timeline at contract acceptance with calendar reminders for every major milestone — as the FSBO seller, you are the transaction coordinator

Action Steps

  1. Consider scheduling a pre-listing inspection now — the $300–$500 cost routinely pays for itself in reduced post-contract negotiation.
  2. Get contractor quotes for any known issues in advance — having quotes ready makes inspection response decisions fast and informed.
  3. Prepare your comp packet for the appraiser: 3–4 recent sold comps that best support your list price, with addresses, sale prices, and square footage.
  4. Know your walk-away number on inspection requests — decide in advance how much total concession (repairs + credits) you're willing to make before you'd rather walk.
  5. Read Module 7 (Negotiation Tactics) alongside this module — inspection negotiation is an extension of offer negotiation and the same principles apply.

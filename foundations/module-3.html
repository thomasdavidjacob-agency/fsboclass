Writing Your Listing

Foundations Course28 min readModule 3 of 6

Your listing description is the single piece of written copy that does the heaviest lifting in your FSBO sale. It appears on the MLS, on Zillow, on Realtor.com, and on every platform that syndicates your listing. It is read by buyers at 11pm on their couch, scrolling through dozens of homes. In that context, your description needs to stop the scroll, create an emotional connection, and motivate a showing appointment — all in about 250 words. This module teaches you how to write one that does exactly that.

Your Listing Copy Is Your First Salesperson

The words in your listing are what drive buyers to schedule showings — and showings lead to offers. A listing agent charges thousands to write this. You're doing it yourself, and doing it right here means more interest, more showings, and a stronger final offer.

Why Most Listing Descriptions Fail

Read any 20 listing descriptions on Zillow in your area and you'll notice an alarming pattern. Most of them read like an inventory manifest: "3BR/2BA home with updated kitchen, hardwood floors, 2-car garage, large backyard, close to schools." That's a bullet list masquerading as a description. It tells buyers nothing they couldn't already see in the listing data fields. It creates no feeling, no aspiration, no urgency.

The homes that generate immediate showing requests are the ones whose descriptions make buyers feel something. They paint a picture of life in the home. They lead with the most compelling feature. They use sensory language. They don't bury the headline in generic adjectives.

Your goal is not to describe every room — buyers will see the photos. Your goal is to make them want to walk through the front door.

The Anatomy of a High-Performing Listing

Element 1: The Headline

Most listing platforms display a headline or property title at the top of your listing. Some platforms (like FSBO.com and Craigslist) give you full control over this. The MLS and Zillow sometimes auto-populate it from the property data, but where you have control, use it.

Your headline should answer the question: "Why is this home special?" Not "3BR/2BA in Maple Grove" — that's a description. A headline is a hook. Examples:

  • "Renovated Craftsman with Chef's Kitchen — Backs to Open Space"
  • "Corner Lot Colonial in Top-Rated School District — Move-In Ready"
  • "Rare Ranch Floor Plan with Finished Walkout Basement — No Stairs Living"

Notice that each headline identifies the home's key differentiator first. What makes your home special? Lead with that.

Element 2: The Opening Sentence

Your first sentence must hook the buyer. Don't open with the address. Don't open with "Welcome to this beautiful home." Open with the home's most compelling benefit — or better yet, a statement that makes the buyer envision themselves living there.

Instead of: "This beautiful 4-bedroom home features an updated kitchen."
Try: "Saturday morning breakfast tastes better in a kitchen like this — vaulted ceilings, quartz countertops, and natural light pouring through the windows overlooking a private backyard."

The second version is longer, but it creates a scene. Buyers buy feelings as much as features.

Element 3: The Feature Body

After your opening hook, you transition to the substance — the key features of your home. The rule here is simple: lead with benefits, not features. A feature is what something is. A benefit is what it means to the buyer.

  • Feature: "2-car garage." Benefit: "Two-car garage keeps both vehicles out of the elements year-round."
  • Feature: "Open floor plan." Benefit: "The open floor plan means the whole family stays connected — whether you're cooking, helping with homework, or watching the game."
  • Feature: "Walk-in closet." Benefit: "A true walk-in primary closet with built-ins — finally, enough space for both of you."

Write 3–5 benefit statements for your home's standout features. These form the body of your description.

Element 4: The Location Close

End your description with a location or lifestyle close — a sentence or two that connects your home to the community, schools, amenities, or lifestyle it enables. This is important because buyers aren't just buying a home; they're buying into a neighborhood and a way of life.

Examples: "Minutes from the Riverwalk, top-rated Lincoln Elementary, and easy highway access for commuters." Or: "Tucked at the end of a quiet cul-de-sac, yet five minutes from the town center."

Element 5: The Call to Action

Finish with a short, direct call to action: "Schedule your private showing today — this one won't last." Or: "Call or text to schedule. Flexible showing hours available." Include your contact phone number or email directly in the description wherever the platform allows it.

What Features to Highlight (By Priority)

Not all features are created equal. Buyers in most markets respond most strongly to the following, in rough order of impact:

  1. Kitchen: Updated kitchen is the single highest-impact feature in most markets. If yours is updated, lead with it.
  2. Primary bedroom/bathroom: The quality of the primary suite drives significant buying decisions, especially for move-up buyers.
  3. Outdoor space: Large lot, private backyard, deck, patio — anything that extends the living space outdoors.
  4. Natural light: Mention specific south- or east-facing orientation, skylights, or large windows if applicable.
  5. Storage: Walk-in closets, ample storage, basement, or attic space — buyers are chronically short on storage.
  6. Recent mechanical updates: New roof, HVAC, water heater, or windows reduce a buyer's anticipated capital expenditure. Mention specific year if recent.
  7. Garage and parking: In suburban and colder markets, garage is highly prioritized. State whether it's attached or detached, heated or not.
  8. Schools: If your home is in a high-rated district, say so explicitly. Many buyers filter by school district before anything else.

Keyword Strategy for the MLS

When your listing syndicates from the MLS to Zillow, Realtor.com, and other platforms, buyers search using keyword filters. Your written description contributes to search indexing. Include these specific terms naturally in your text:

  • Your neighborhood name (not just city)
  • School names ("Lincoln Elementary," "Westview High")
  • Nearby landmarks or attractions buyers care about
  • Specific material upgrades ("quartz," "hardwood," "stainless steel")
  • Structural features buyers search for ("single story," "no stairs," "finished basement," "main floor primary")

Do not keyword-stuff. Write naturally and include these terms where they make sense. Buyers read descriptions and a stuffed paragraph reads as amateurish and desperate.

What Photos to Lead With

Your listing photos appear in order. Most buyers see the first photo before they read a single word of your description. The order of your photos is a strategic decision, not a logistical one.

The Optimal Photo Order

  1. Photo 1: Exterior front — clean, well-lit, ideally blue-sky day. This is your hero image.
  2. Photos 2–3: Your most impressive interior space. Usually the kitchen or living room.
  3. Photos 4–5: Primary bedroom and bathroom.
  4. Photos 6–8: Remaining bedrooms, secondary bathrooms.
  5. Photos 9–10: Backyard, deck/patio, outdoor features.
  6. Photos 11+: Garage, laundry, basement, mechanical room, additional storage.
  7. Final photos: Neighborhood, street scene, proximity to parks or amenities.

Never lead with a bathroom, a bedroom closet, or a photo of the street. Lead with strength. Buyers form impressions in seconds, and the first three photos determine whether they keep scrolling or move on.

The Photography Non-Negotiable

Smartphone photos are not acceptable for a home listing unless taken by someone who understands real estate photography angles, lighting, and staging. Dark, cluttered, or poorly composed photos will cost you more in lost buyer interest than a professional photographer charges. Budget $200–$400 for a professional shoot. It is the highest-ROI investment in your FSBO sale.

Staging Basics Before the Shoot

Your listing photos need to be taken in a home that is staged — not just clean, but intentionally arranged to photograph well and appeal to the broadest possible buyer pool. Here's the minimum staging checklist before your photo shoot:

  • Remove all personal photos, family memorabilia, and highly personal décor
  • Clear kitchen countertops of appliances, dishes, mail, and clutter — leave only 1–2 decorative items
  • Remove excessive furniture (most rooms have too much — less is more for photos)
  • Replace any burned-out light bulbs — bright rooms photograph larger
  • Make all beds with fresh, neutral linens
  • Remove items from shower floors, tubs, and toilet tops in bathrooms
  • Open all blinds and window treatments — maximize natural light
  • Remove pet items (bowls, beds, toys, litter boxes) from all visible areas
  • Clear the garage enough that at least one car can fit in the frame
  • Mow the lawn, trim hedges, remove garden hoses and tools from the yard

Common Listing Description Mistakes

Mistake 1: Writing in the Third Person About Your Own Home

"This charming home offers..." sounds like you're describing someone else's property. It's detached and impersonal. Either write in the second person ("Your new home features...") or use active, engaging language without a subject ("Vaulted ceilings and original hardwood throughout the main floor...").

Mistake 2: Overusing Meaningless Adjectives

"Gorgeous," "stunning," "beautiful," "amazing," "wonderful" — these words appear in virtually every listing and mean nothing. Replace them with specific, verifiable details. Don't say "gorgeous kitchen." Say "kitchen with custom cabinetry, quartzite countertops, and a 36-inch range."

Mistake 3: Listing Obvious Features

Don't describe things buyers can already see in the data fields. If your listing shows 4 bedrooms, don't write "four bedrooms." Use those words for something that adds value — how large the bedrooms are, whether any have walk-in closets, or how the layout positions them for privacy.

Mistake 4: Mentioning Problems

Never describe defects or issues in your listing description. You'll disclose them properly on your seller disclosure forms. Your listing description is marketing copy — it should present your home's genuine best features honestly. "Needs some TLC" or "sold as-is" in the listing description immediately signals problems and reduces your buyer pool.

Mistake 5: Failing to Mention FSBO Status Clearly

On platforms like Zillow FSBO, your status is already visible. But in the description itself, it helps to clarify contact instructions: "Owner-seller — call or text [number] for showings." This sets expectations for buyer's agents who may reach out regarding commission structure.

Sample Listing Description Framework

Hook: "Weekend mornings start different when your kitchen looks like this."
Feature benefits: 3–4 sentences on standout features with benefit framing.
Supporting features: 2–3 bullet points for secondary features.
Location close: 1–2 sentences on neighborhood, schools, proximity.
CTA: "Call [number] to schedule your private tour today."
Total length: 200–300 words.

Platform Character Limits and What They Mean for Your Copy

One of the most overlooked aspects of listing copy strategy is that different platforms impose very different character limits on your description — and they're character limits, not word limits. A single comma counts as a character. Understanding these limits before you write protects you from having your strongest copy truncated at exactly the wrong moment.

Character Limits by Platform

  • MLS public remarks field: Typically 500–1,000 characters depending on your regional MLS. Your flat-fee MLS service will specify the exact limit when you submit your listing. At 800 characters, you have roughly 120–140 words to work with — enough for a focused hook, three strong benefit statements, and a CTA.
  • Zillow public description: 2,000 characters. This is your primary writing canvas and where you should invest the most effort. At this limit, you can write a full, layered description with strong hooks, benefit framing, secondary features, a location close, and complete contact information.
  • Facebook Marketplace: 5,000 characters. Facebook's generous limit lets you go deeper — multiple sections, room-by-room highlights, neighborhood context, and full showing instructions. Many FSBO sellers underuse this and paste in the same 250-word MLS description. Write a longer, more conversational version for Facebook.
  • Realtor.com: Syndicates from your MLS listing rather than accepting a separate submission, so whatever you enter in your MLS public remarks is what appears on Realtor.com — sometimes with further truncation in the preview card display.

The Two-Version Strategy

Write your full listing description targeting Zillow's 2,000-character limit. Make it your best work — complete hook, full benefit copy, location close, CTA with contact number. Then create a tighter MLS version: strip it down to your strongest opening sentence, your three best benefit statements in condensed form, and your CTA. Remove adjectives that don't add information, cut redundant details, and eliminate any generic location language that wastes characters ("conveniently located near shopping and restaurants" tells buyers nothing specific).

A Practical Character-Count Exercise

Paste your draft description into a free character counter (search "character counter" — multiple free tools exist online). Identify your character count, then work through it with this priority order for cuts: first, remove weak adjectives that duplicate what photos already show. Second, cut generic statements buyers can infer from the listing data (bedroom count, city name). Third, tighten compound sentences into lean, direct ones. What remains after those cuts is your MLS version. What you had before is your Zillow version.

A Real Before/After Listing Description

The difference between a generic listing and a compelling one becomes immediately clear when you see them side by side. Here is an actual before/after example using the same property and the same facts — only the execution differs.

The Weak Version (Before)

"Beautiful 4BR/2BA home with updated kitchen and large backyard. New roof in 2022. Close to schools and shopping. Two-car garage. Open floor plan. Large master suite with walk-in closet. Call for showing."

The Strong Version (After)

"Saturday mornings in this kitchen — quartz countertops, farmhouse sink, 36-inch range, and windows that fill the space with east-facing light — make it hard to leave. Four bedrooms including a vaulted primary suite with a true walk-in closet, main-floor laundry, and a private half-acre lot that backs to no rear neighbors. New roof (2022), new HVAC (2021). Walk to Lincoln Elementary; commuters are 8 minutes to I-5. Owner-seller: call or text [number] to schedule your private showing — flexible hours including evenings and weekends."

What Makes the Second Version Work

The difference between these two descriptions isn't length — it's execution. Here's exactly what the strong version does that the weak version doesn't:

  • Sensory and scene-setting opening: "Saturday mornings in this kitchen" places the reader inside the home doing something real. It's not a description of the kitchen — it's an experience of it. The weak version's "updated kitchen" is a label; the strong version's description is a feeling.
  • Specific proof points instead of vague claims: "Quartz countertops, farmhouse sink, 36-inch range" gives buyers verifiable detail. "Updated kitchen" means nothing — updated how? When? With what materials? Specific details build confidence and justify the price.
  • Benefit framing throughout: "Main-floor laundry" is a feature. "A private half-acre lot that backs to no rear neighbors" is a benefit — it tells the buyer exactly why that lot matters to their daily life and their privacy.
  • Year-specific mechanical updates: "New roof (2022), new HVAC (2021)" gives buyers exact data that reduces their fear of capital expenditures. It's more credible than "newer systems."
  • Precise commute data: "8 minutes to I-5" is information buyers can validate and act on. "Close to shopping" is meaningless noise.
  • Clear FSBO identification and direct CTA: "Owner-seller: call or text [number]" signals the FSBO status, establishes direct contact, and removes ambiguity about who to reach and how. "Call for showing" is passive; "flexible hours including evenings and weekends" proactively removes friction.

Writing for Different Buyer Profiles

The most effective listing descriptions speak directly to the most likely buyer for your specific home. A description optimized for a first-time buyer emphasizes different features than one aimed at a downsizing empty nester or a move-up family. Before you write a word, ask yourself: who is most likely to buy this home, and what do they care most about?

First-Time Buyers

First-time buyers are typically in the lower-to-middle price range, often buying their first home out of a rental. They prioritize: move-in readiness (they don't want a project), clear school district identification (many are starting or planning families), storage (coming from apartments, they're acutely aware of what they don't have enough of), and reassurance about mechanical condition. For a home that skews toward first-time buyer appeal, lean into move-in readiness, name the school district explicitly, mention any recently replaced systems, and keep the description warm and approachable in tone.

Move-Up Buyers

Move-up buyers are trading their starter home for something bigger or better. They know what they want because they've lived without it: a better kitchen, a real primary suite, space for entertaining, a dedicated home office, a garage that actually fits two cars. For this buyer, focus your copy on kitchen quality and specific details, primary suite specifics (square footage if notable, closet configuration, en-suite bathroom features), and any entertainment-focused spaces like a finished basement, large deck, or open-plan living area.

Empty Nesters and Downsizers

Buyers in this profile are often moving from larger homes and prioritizing simplicity, accessibility, and proximity to amenities. They care about: single-level living or main-floor primary suite, low-maintenance yard, proximity to medical care, dining, cultural amenities, and walkability. For this buyer, explicitly mention if your home is single-story or has a main-floor primary. Mention lot size and any low-maintenance landscaping. Include walkability and proximity to specific amenities they value.

Write for Your Most Likely Buyer

Don't try to appeal to everyone in a single description — you'll end up appealing to no one in particular. Identify the most likely buyer for your home based on price range, size, and features, then write copy that speaks directly to their priorities. The most compelling listings feel personal, not generic. Buyers should read your description and think "this was written for someone like me."

The MLS Remarks vs. Public Description Difference

When your flat-fee MLS service enters your listing into the MLS, there are typically two separate description fields available to you. Understanding the difference between them — and using each one strategically — is something most FSBO sellers miss entirely.

Agent Remarks (Private Field)

The agent remarks field (sometimes called "confidential remarks" or "broker remarks") is visible only to licensed real estate agents accessing the listing through the MLS system. It does not appear on Zillow, Realtor.com, or any public-facing platform. This is where you communicate professional, operational information to buyer's agents who will be showing the property. Use this field to cover:

  • Detailed showing instructions (lockbox location and code format, how to schedule, minimum notice required)
  • HOA contact information and any upcoming HOA meetings or assessments
  • Your flat-fee MLS broker's contact information for agents who need to reach a licensed party
  • The buyer's agent commission you are offering (if any), stated clearly
  • Any special terms of the sale (items excluded, preferred closing date, occupancy needs)
  • Information about ongoing work or recent improvements agents should communicate to buyers
Sample Agent Remarks

"FSBO — owner handles all showings and negotiations directly. Supra lockbox on front door; access code provided via ShowingTime. 2-hour advance notice required. Owner-paid buyer's agent commission: 2.5%. Contact listing broker [flat-fee service name and phone] for MLS-related inquiries only. Preferred close: 45 days. Washer/dryer excluded from sale. Seller disclosure, pre-inspection report, and HOA docs available upon request."

Public Remarks (Buyer-Facing Field)

The public remarks field is your marketing copy — what syndicated to Zillow, Realtor.com, and every consumer platform. This is where everything from this module applies: sensory hooks, benefit framing, specific proof points, location close, and CTA. Keep this field entirely buyer-focused. Don't include showing instructions, commission details, or agent-directed language in your public remarks — it's noise to buyers and looks unprofessional.

Sample Public Remarks

"Saturday mornings in this kitchen — quartz countertops, farmhouse sink, 36-inch range, and east-facing windows that flood the space with morning light — make it hard to leave. Four bedrooms including a vaulted primary suite with walk-in closet, main-floor laundry, and a private half-acre lot backing to no rear neighbors. New roof (2022), new HVAC (2021), updated electrical panel (2020). Lincoln Elementary attendance area. Eight minutes to I-5 for commuters. Owner-seller: call or text [number] for a private showing — evenings and weekends available."

The discipline of keeping these two fields separate — professional logistics in agent remarks, buyer-facing marketing in public remarks — makes you look like a sophisticated seller who knows what they're doing. It also ensures buyer's agents can find the information they need without cluttering the copy that buyers see.

Key Takeaways

  • Your listing description's job is to motivate a showing — not to inventory every room
  • Lead with your home's single most compelling feature as the headline and opening hook
  • Write in benefits, not features: tell buyers what each feature means for their daily life
  • MLS remarks are typically 500–1,000 characters; Zillow allows 2,000; Facebook allows 5,000 — write a full version for Zillow and a tight version for the MLS
  • The agent remarks field is for operational information (showing instructions, commission, HOA) — keep buyer-facing marketing in public remarks only
  • Identify your most likely buyer type and write copy that speaks directly to their specific priorities — don't try to appeal to everyone
  • Specific, verifiable details beat vague adjectives every time: "quartz countertops, 36-inch range" beats "gorgeous kitchen"
  • The first three photos determine whether buyers keep scrolling — lead with strength, always the exterior first
  • Professional photography is the highest-ROI investment in your FSBO — budget $200–$400 and don't skip it
  • Stage before you shoot: clear countertops, open blinds, remove personal items, maximize natural light

Action Steps

  1. Identify your most likely buyer profile (first-time buyer, move-up buyer, or downsizer) and write a one-sentence description of what they care most about in a home.
  2. Write your listing headline — identify your home's single strongest differentiator and lead with it.
  3. Draft your full Zillow listing description (target 1,800–2,000 characters) using the framework: hook → feature benefits → supporting details → location close → CTA. Count characters and adjust.
  4. Create your tighter MLS version (target 700–900 characters) by cutting adjectives, generic statements, and redundant details from your Zillow draft.
  5. Draft your agent remarks covering showing instructions, commission offer, flat-fee broker contact, and any special sale terms.
  6. Complete your staging checklist before booking the photography shoot.
  7. Book a professional photographer — search "[your city] real estate photographer" and budget $200–$400.
  8. Review your final description for meaningless adjectives, obvious features, and anything that might signal a problem — cut or replace anything that doesn't add value.

Understanding Purchase Contracts

Foundations Course35 min readModule 5 of 6

The purchase contract is the legal document that governs your home sale from the moment an offer is accepted to the day of closing. It is typically 8–15 pages of dense legal language, written by attorneys for real estate boards, and designed to protect both parties from every scenario imaginable. Most FSBO sellers read it for the first time when an offer arrives — which is exactly the wrong time to encounter it. This module walks you through every key clause in plain language so you're never caught off guard by something you didn't understand.

This Module Is Worth $13,500

Understanding every clause in a purchase contract is the core thing you pay a listing agent to do. On a $450,000 home, their 3% fee is $13,500. This module puts that knowledge directly in your hands — and you keep the fee.

What Is a Purchase Contract?

A residential purchase contract (also called a purchase and sale agreement, or PSA) is a legally binding contract between you (the seller) and the buyer that specifies the terms of the sale. Once both parties sign, you are under contract and the closing process begins. Breaking a signed purchase contract has legal and financial consequences for either party.

Most states have a standard form purchase contract developed by the state's real estate association or attorney general's office. Buyer's agents typically use the standard form for their state. As an FSBO seller, you should download your state's standard purchase contract form and read it in full before you receive your first offer. Familiarity with the document removes the stress of encountering it for the first time under negotiation pressure.

Where to Get Your State's Contract

Search "[your state] residential purchase agreement PDF" or visit your state's real estate commission website. In many states, the form used by buyer's agents is published publicly. Alternatively, your title company or real estate attorney can provide the standard form used in your area.

Key Contract Elements Explained

Purchase Price and Earnest Money

The purchase price is the amount the buyer is offering for your home. This is the starting point for negotiation — it may not be your asking price, and you're not obligated to accept it as written.

Earnest money (also called a good faith deposit) is a sum the buyer deposits — typically within 1–3 business days of acceptance — to demonstrate their seriousness. Earnest money is typically 1–3% of the purchase price and is held in escrow by the title company or your state's approved escrow holder until closing, at which point it's applied to the buyer's down payment or closing costs.

What happens to earnest money if the deal falls apart depends on which party backs out and whether they had contractual grounds to do so. If the buyer backs out without a contractual contingency to protect them, you generally keep the earnest money. If they back out due to a valid contingency (inspection issues, financing failure, etc.), they typically get it back.

Closing Date

The closing date is when the transaction officially completes — when you transfer title and the buyer gets the keys. Most purchase contracts specify a closing date 30–45 days from the contract acceptance date, though cash sales can close in as few as 7–14 days and some financed transactions extend to 60 days.

The closing date is negotiable. If you need more time to find your next home, you can negotiate a later closing. If the buyer needs to close quickly, you can accommodate that in exchange for price or other concessions. Closing date flexibility is a negotiating chip on both sides.

Possession Date

Possession date is when the buyer takes physical possession of the property — when they get the keys. This is often (but not always) the same as the closing date. It is possible to negotiate a possession date that is days or even weeks after closing, allowing you more time to vacate. This arrangement is called a "post-closing occupancy agreement" or "rent-back" and is covered in more detail in Module 7.

The Three Major Contingencies

Contingencies are conditions that must be satisfied for the contract to proceed. If a contingency is not met, the buyer typically has the right to exit the contract and recover their earnest money. Understanding contingencies is essential — they are the primary source of deals falling apart.

1. Financing Contingency

The financing contingency (also called a mortgage contingency) protects the buyer if they cannot secure a loan to purchase your home. It gives the buyer a specified number of days (typically 21–30) to obtain a firm loan commitment from their lender. If the lender declines the loan for any reason, the buyer can exit the contract without penalty.

As a seller, you want this period to be as short as possible and to require the buyer to act diligently. Some buyers offer to waive the financing contingency (common in all-cash or very strong offers) — this is favorable to you but increases the buyer's risk.

2. Inspection Contingency

The inspection contingency gives the buyer the right to have the home professionally inspected within a specified timeframe (typically 7–14 days of acceptance). After reviewing the inspection report, the buyer can:

  • Accept the property as-is and proceed
  • Request that you make specific repairs before closing
  • Request a price reduction in lieu of repairs
  • Request a credit at closing to cover repair costs
  • Exit the contract entirely if they're unsatisfied with the inspection results

Inspection negotiations are where a disproportionate number of deals stall or fall apart. Module 10 (Complete tier) covers inspection negotiation strategy in depth.

3. Appraisal Contingency

If the buyer is using financing, the lender will require an independent appraisal of the property. The appraisal contingency protects the buyer if the home appraises for less than the purchase price. In this scenario, the lender will only finance up to the appraised value — the buyer must either make up the difference in cash, renegotiate the price with you, or exit the contract.

Appraisal contingencies can be waived by buyers willing to make up any gap between the purchase price and the appraised value. In hot markets with multiple offers, buyers sometimes waive the appraisal contingency to strengthen their offer. Understand what this means for you: if the home appraises short and the buyer has waived the appraisal contingency, they're committed to purchasing at the agreed price regardless.

Other Important Contract Clauses

Included and Excluded Items

The purchase contract specifies what's included in the sale (fixtures, appliances, window treatments) and what's explicitly excluded (the dining room chandelier you're taking to your new home, the garage refrigerator, the mounted TV in the bedroom). Be specific. What's attached to the home is generally considered a fixture and included in the sale by default — but you can exclude items by naming them. It's far better to address inclusions and exclusions explicitly upfront than to have a dispute on moving day.

Sale of Buyer's Current Home (Home Sale Contingency)

Some buyers condition their purchase on selling their own home first. This creates a chain of transactions — if their sale falls through, yours does too. Home sale contingencies are generally unfavorable to sellers in markets with multiple buyers. In slower markets, they're more common. If you accept an offer with a home sale contingency, consider including a "kick-out clause" that allows you to continue marketing the home and gives the buyer a window (typically 48–72 hours) to waive the contingency or lose the contract if you receive a better offer.

Seller Disclosures and Representations

The contract will typically reference the seller's disclosure form as a part of the agreement. Anything you know about the property's condition that could affect its value or the buyer's decision must be disclosed. Failure to disclose known defects can expose you to legal liability after closing. Module 9 (Complete tier) covers disclosure requirements in detail by state.

Default and Remedies

Every purchase contract includes provisions for what happens if either party defaults. If the buyer defaults without a valid contingency, you typically keep the earnest money as liquidated damages. If you default as the seller — by refusing to sell or accepting another offer after you're under contract — the buyer may be entitled to specific performance (a court forcing the sale) or damages. Don't enter into a contract you're not fully committed to completing.

Attorney Review Period

Some states (New Jersey, New York, Connecticut, Illinois, Massachusetts, and others) require or allow a period — typically 3–5 business days after contract signing — during which either party's attorney can review the contract and propose modifications or void the agreement. If you're in one of these states, be aware that a signed contract is not final until the attorney review period has expired.

Reviewing an Offer You've Received

When you receive an offer, don't react emotionally to the number. Evaluate the entire offer as a package. Consider:

  • Net to you after concessions: A $410,000 offer with $10,000 in closing cost assistance nets you $400,000. A $405,000 offer with no concessions nets more.
  • Financing vs. cash: Cash offers close faster, eliminate appraisal risk, and fall through less often. They're generally preferable even at a slightly lower price.
  • Contingencies: Fewer contingencies mean less risk that the deal collapses. Weigh contingency risk in your evaluation.
  • Closing date flexibility: Does the proposed closing date work for you? Does it give you enough time to find your next home?
  • Earnest money amount: A higher earnest money deposit signals buyer seriousness and increases the cost to them if they back out without cause.
Use a Real Estate Attorney, Not Just the Contract

In most states, hiring a real estate attorney to review your contract is not required but is strongly recommended for FSBO sellers. Attorney fees for a transaction review typically run $500–$1,500 — a small cost relative to your transaction size. In states that use attorneys for closing rather than title companies, an attorney is already part of the standard process.

Understanding Purchase Contract Addenda

Addenda are supplemental pages that attach to and become part of the base purchase agreement. In many transactions, the addenda stack is as long as or longer than the core contract itself. As an FSBO seller, you'll encounter addenda prepared by buyer's agents, by lenders, and required by federal law — and you need to know what each one does before you sign.

Common Addenda You'll Encounter

  • Inspection Addendum: Specifies the inspection timeline (usually 7–14 days), the process for submitting an inspection response, and the seller's obligations regarding any requested repairs. Some inspection addenda include a specific dollar cap — for example, the seller is not obligated to make repairs exceeding $X in total cost. Know where your contract sets this threshold.
  • Financing Addendum: Details the loan type (conventional, FHA, VA, USDA), loan amount, maximum interest rate the buyer will accept, and the deadline by which they must obtain a firm loan commitment. This is one of the most important addenda to read carefully — it defines the buyer's exit rights if their financing falls through and the timeline within which those rights apply.
  • Lead Paint Disclosure Addendum: A federal requirement for all homes built before 1978. Both you and the buyer must sign this form acknowledging the potential presence of lead-based paint. You must disclose any known lead paint hazards and provide any existing inspection reports. Failure to comply with this federal requirement can result in civil and criminal penalties.
  • Short Sale Addendum: If you owe more than your home is worth and your lender must approve the sale price, this addendum outlines the process, timeline, and the buyer's rights if lender approval is delayed or denied. Most FSBO sellers won't need this, but it's critical to understand if you're selling underwater.
  • Seller Post-Close Occupancy Addendum: If you need to remain in the home after closing (rent-back), this addendum governs that arrangement — daily rent rate, security deposit, insurance responsibility, and what happens if you fail to vacate on time. Covered in depth in the section below.

How Addenda Are Incorporated

Addenda are referenced in the body of the main contract — typically in a clause that reads something like "The following addenda are attached hereto and incorporated herein by reference." Once both parties sign the addendum, it carries the same legal weight as the main contract. A term in an addendum that conflicts with the main contract body typically controls — meaning the addendum language prevails. This is why it's critical to read every addendum before signing, not just the main contract pages.

Red flag to watch: addenda that modify the buyer's contingency rights in buyer-favorable ways without corresponding seller benefit. Examples include extending the inspection period significantly beyond the standard, adding a general "buyer satisfaction" clause that allows exit for any reason, or weakening the earnest money forfeiture provision. Your real estate attorney should flag any non-standard addendum language before you countersign.

Earnest Money: Mechanics, Disputes, and Protection

Earnest money is frequently misunderstood by FSBO sellers — both its function during the transaction and the process for resolving disputes if the deal falls apart. Understanding exactly how earnest money works before you're in a dispute is the difference between recovering what you're owed and losing it to procedural confusion.

Who Holds It and How It's Deposited

In states that use title companies for closing — which covers most of the country — the earnest money is held in escrow by the title company in a dedicated trust account. In attorney-closing states (Massachusetts, New York, New Jersey, Connecticut, Georgia, South Carolina, and others), it's typically held by the buyer's attorney or a neutral third party. The buyer deposits earnest money by wire transfer or certified check, and you should receive written confirmation from the title company within 24 hours of the deposit deadline. If you don't receive that confirmation, follow up immediately — an earnest money deposit that wasn't actually made is a serious problem that needs to be addressed before the transaction proceeds further.

Standard contracts require deposit within 1–3 business days of contract acceptance. Confirm the exact deadline in your contract and verify directly with the title company that funds were received on time. Don't rely on the buyer's agent to relay this confirmation.

What Happens at Closing

If the transaction closes as planned, the earnest money is applied to the buyer's obligations at closing — typically as part of the down payment or to cover closing costs. It reduces the amount the buyer needs to bring to closing day. From your perspective as the seller, it moves through the title company's books without requiring any action from you.

What Happens When the Deal Falls Through

Releasing earnest money after a failed transaction requires either (a) a written release agreement signed by both the buyer and the seller, or (b) a court order or arbitration decision. Title companies will not release funds based on one party's claim alone — they require mutual written consent or a legal determination. This means that if you believe you're entitled to the earnest money and the buyer disagrees, you may be looking at a legal process to resolve it. The scenarios below cover the most common situations:

Scenario Who Gets the Earnest Money Why
Buyer exits within the inspection contingency period citing inspection findings Buyer Valid contingency exercised — buyer is protected by contract terms
Buyer cannot obtain financing within the financing contingency period and provides lender denial letter Buyer Valid contingency exercised — buyer is protected by contract terms
Buyer backs out after all contingencies have expired with no contractual grounds Seller Buyer defaulted without a valid contingency — earnest money is liquidated damages per contract
Seller refuses to close, removes the home from the transaction, or accepts another offer while under contract Buyer recovers earnest money; seller may face additional liability Seller defaulted — buyer may seek specific performance or sue for actual damages beyond the earnest money amount

Post-Closing Occupancy Agreements (Rent-Back)

One of the most common timing challenges FSBO sellers face is the gap between when they close on their current home and when they take possession of their next one. A post-closing occupancy agreement — also called a seller rent-back or leaseback — solves this problem by allowing you to remain in your home after closing as a tenant of the new owner, typically for a period of days or weeks.

How It Works

At closing, you transfer title and receive your proceeds as planned. However, instead of vacating immediately, you remain in the home under a separate occupancy agreement. You pay rent to the new owner — typically calculated as a daily rate based on the buyer's principal, interest, taxes, and insurance (PITI) payment — and continue to maintain the home in the same condition as at closing. The occupancy period is defined in the addendum, along with a specific move-out date and the consequences if you don't vacate on time.

Lender Restrictions on Rent-Back Periods

This is the detail most sellers miss, and it matters significantly. The buyer's lender imposes limits on how long a rent-back can run based on the loan type:

  • Conventional loans (primary residence): Most conventional lenders limit rent-backs to 60 days. The buyer must intend to occupy the home as a primary residence, and a lengthy rent-back can conflict with that intent.
  • FHA loans: FHA guidelines require the buyer to take occupancy within 60 days of closing. Rent-backs that prevent occupancy beyond this window can jeopardize the loan.
  • VA loans: VA loans are the most restrictive — the veteran-borrower must intend to personally occupy the property, and extended seller occupancy can conflict with VA occupancy requirements and trigger loan fraud concerns if not handled properly.

What to Include in the Agreement

A well-drafted post-closing occupancy addendum covers:

  • The daily rent rate and how it's calculated
  • A security deposit (typically one month's equivalent rent) held by the title company until you vacate
  • Who maintains the property during the occupancy period and who is responsible for damage
  • Which party carries insurance — your homeowner's policy typically becomes invalid at closing, so you'll need renter's insurance during the rent-back period
  • The exact vacate date and the financial penalty (typically per-day holdover rent, often at double the standard rate) if you remain beyond that date
Disclose the Rent-Back to the Buyer's Lender Early

Extended rent-back arrangements must be disclosed to the buyer's lender before the purchase contract is finalized — not at the last minute before closing. Lenders that discover an undisclosed rent-back arrangement after underwriting may delay or deny the loan. If you know you'll need a rent-back period, disclose it early, keep it within the lender's allowed window, and ensure the post-closing occupancy addendum is drafted and shared with the buyer's lender as part of the loan package.

Decoding an Escalation Clause

In competitive markets — any market where homes routinely receive multiple offers within the first few days of listing — you may receive offers that include an escalation clause. Understanding exactly how to evaluate, trigger, and compare escalation clauses is essential to extracting maximum value when multiple buyers compete for your home.

How an Escalation Clause Works

An escalation clause is a provision within an offer that says, in effect: "We offer $X, but if another bona fide offer exceeds our bid, we'll automatically increase our offer to $Y above that competing offer, up to a maximum of $Z." The three numbers that define any escalation clause are:

  • The starting bid: The price the buyer is willing to pay without competition
  • The escalation increment: How much above a competing offer they'll automatically go
  • The ceiling price: The absolute maximum they'll pay under any circumstance

Example: "Buyer offers $415,000 with an escalation of $2,500 above any bona fide competing offer, up to a maximum of $432,500." If you receive a second offer at $418,000, this escalation clause triggers to $420,500. If the second offer is at $430,000, the clause triggers to $432,500 — the ceiling — even though $2,500 above $430,000 would be $432,500, which happens to equal the ceiling in this case.

How to Trigger an Escalation Clause

You can trigger an escalation clause only with a real, documented competing offer. You must provide the buyer with a redacted copy of the competing offer showing the competing purchase price — the buyer is entitled to verify that the competing offer is genuine. You may redact the competing buyer's personal information (name, contact details) but not the price. Some buyers' agents will request to see the competing offer before accepting the escalation; this is a reasonable and standard request.

When Multiple Buyers Submit Escalation Clauses

Compare the ceiling prices across all escalation offers — the buyer with the highest ceiling is your price target in a best-case scenario. However, ceiling price alone doesn't determine the best offer. A buyer whose escalation tops out at $435,000 with a financing contingency may be less valuable than a cash buyer whose ceiling is $430,000. Evaluate the full offer terms alongside the escalation mechanics.

Call for Highest and Best to Eliminate Escalation Complexity

If you receive multiple offers with escalation clauses and the comparisons become complicated, you have a straightforward alternative: issue a "call for highest and best offers" with a deadline. Ask all buyers to submit their final, best offer by a specific date and time — no escalation clauses, just a firm number with final terms. This eliminates the complexity of comparing multiple escalation scenarios and often produces higher prices than escalation clauses would have generated, because buyers who are serious must commit to their true ceiling without the psychological safety net of the escalation mechanism.

Key Takeaways

  • Read your state's standard purchase contract before you receive your first offer — familiarity removes negotiation pressure
  • Earnest money protects you if the buyer backs out without a valid contingency — 1–3% of purchase price is typical
  • The three major contingencies are financing, inspection, and appraisal — each gives the buyer an exit ramp under specific conditions
  • Evaluate offers as a complete package: net price after concessions, financing type, contingencies, and closing date flexibility all matter
  • Include and exclusion items must be specified explicitly — what's attached is generally included unless named otherwise
  • A real estate attorney review of your contract costs $500–$1,500 and is worth every dollar for FSBO sellers
  • Addenda carry the same legal weight as the main contract — read every addendum before signing, and have your attorney flag non-standard language
  • Earnest money disputes require written mutual consent or a court order to resolve — understand the four common scenarios before they happen
  • Rent-back periods are subject to strict lender limitations — disclose any rent-back arrangement to the buyer's lender before contract finalization
  • Escalation clauses can only be triggered with a documented competing offer — or issue a "highest and best" call to eliminate the complexity entirely

Action Steps

  1. Download your state's standard residential purchase agreement and read it in full before you list.
  2. Identify a real estate attorney in your area who handles residential closings — have their contact information ready before you receive an offer.
  3. Make a list of all items you plan to exclude from the sale (specific appliances, fixtures, or personal property) so you can address them clearly in any negotiation.
  4. Decide your position on contingencies in advance: what contingencies are you willing to accept, and what would cause you to push back?
  5. Complete Module 6 to understand what happens after you accept an offer — knowing the full closing process helps you negotiate closing dates and timelines confidently.

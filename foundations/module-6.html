The Closing Process

Foundations Course30 min readModule 6 of 6

You've accepted an offer. You're under contract. Now what? The closing process is the 30–60 day period between contract acceptance and the day you hand over the keys and receive your proceeds. It involves multiple parties, dozens of tasks, and a significant amount of paperwork — and as an FSBO seller, you're coordinating much of it yourself. This module gives you a complete timeline, explains the role of every party involved, and prepares you for closing day so nothing surprises you.

The Finish Line — And Your Payoff

The closing table is where commissions are traditionally collected. As an FSBO seller who makes it to closing, the listing commission stays with you — typically $10,000–$15,000 or more depending on your sale price.

The Closing Timeline: Day by Day

The closing process follows a relatively predictable sequence. Here's what typically happens in each phase of the 30–45 day window:

Days 1–3: Contract Executed and Earnest Money Deposited

Both parties sign the purchase contract. The buyer deposits earnest money — typically within 1–3 business days — into escrow with the title company. You should receive written confirmation that the deposit has been received. If the buyer misses the earnest money deadline without explanation, that's a red flag that warrants attention.

Days 1–7: Title Company/Attorney Opens Escrow

The title company (or real estate attorney in attorney-closing states) opens an escrow file. This is the neutral third party that holds funds and documents, coordinates the transaction, and issues title insurance. You'll receive a contact name and file number — keep this information handy for the duration of the transaction.

At this stage, the title company begins the title search — a review of public records to verify that you actually own the property, that there are no liens or encumbrances that would prevent you from selling it with clear title, and that no other parties have claims to the property.

Days 5–14: Home Inspection

The buyer schedules a professional home inspection. The inspector will spend 2–4 hours examining every accessible component of the home: foundation, roof, electrical, plumbing, HVAC, windows, insulation, and more. You don't need to be present, but you do need to provide access.

After the inspection, the buyer receives a report — typically 40–80 pages — and has a window (usually 7–14 days) to submit an inspection response. This response may request repairs, credits, or price reductions. Module 10 covers inspection negotiation in detail.

Days 14–21: Appraisal (Financed Purchases)

For financed purchases, the buyer's lender orders an appraisal. You don't select the appraiser — the lender assigns one from a rotating pool. The appraiser will schedule an appointment to visit the home, measure square footage, note condition, and compare it to recent sold properties to determine market value.

The appraisal result determines whether the lender will fund the loan at the agreed purchase price. If the appraiser values the home below the purchase price, you'll need to negotiate — either reduce the price, have the buyer make up the gap in cash, or a combination of both. If the appraisal comes in at or above the purchase price, you proceed.

Days 20–30: Loan Underwriting and Approval

The buyer's lender is simultaneously processing the loan application. Underwriting involves verifying the buyer's income, employment, assets, and debt — a process that involves considerable documentation and occasional unexpected requests. As the seller, your role here is minimal — but be responsive to any requests from the title company for additional documentation.

Common causes of underwriting delays: buyer changes jobs, makes a large purchase, or opens new credit during this period. All of these can jeopardize loan approval. Your transaction coordinator or title company will keep you informed of loan status milestones.

Days 25–30: Final Walkthrough

Buyers are typically entitled to a final walkthrough of the property within 24–48 hours of closing. The purpose is to verify that the home is in substantially the same condition as when they made the offer, that any agreed-upon repairs have been completed, and that all included items are still present.

This is not a second inspection — it's a condition check. Prepare for the final walkthrough by ensuring the home is clean, all personal property you're keeping has been removed, all agreed repairs are completed with receipts available, and all included appliances and fixtures are present and functional.

Days 28–45: Closing Day

Closing typically takes place at the title company's office (or real estate attorney's office in some states), though remote and mobile closings are increasingly available. The buyer and seller may sign simultaneously at the same table, or separately at different times during the day.

What You Sign at Closing

As the seller, you'll execute a smaller set of documents than the buyer (who signs the mortgage loan package). Here's what to expect:

  • Deed: The most important document. This transfers legal ownership of the property from you to the buyer. A closing officer or notary witnesses your signature. The deed is recorded with the county after closing.
  • Settlement Statement (ALTA/CD): A detailed accounting of all credits, debits, prorations, fees, and the final distribution of funds. Review this carefully — errors happen. You'll typically receive a preliminary version 24–48 hours before closing for review.
  • Seller's Affidavit: A sworn statement confirming that you're legally entitled to sell the property, that there are no undisclosed liens or judgments, and that you've occupied the home as represented.
  • Payoff Authorization: If you have a mortgage, this authorizes the title company to pay it off from your proceeds.
  • 1099-S Tax Form: The title company is required to report the sale to the IRS. You'll sign this at closing. Your tax advisor uses it when preparing your return — see Module 16 for tax implications.

How Funds Are Transferred

On closing day, money moves in a very specific sequence:

  1. The buyer's lender wires the loan amount to the title company
  2. The buyer wires their down payment and closing costs to the title company
  3. The title company verifies that all funds are received
  4. Documents are signed and the deed is sent for recording
  5. Once recording is confirmed, the title company disburses funds: pays off your mortgage, pays transfer taxes, pays all vendors, and sends your net proceeds

Your net proceeds arrive either as a check handed to you at closing or, more commonly, wired to your bank account. If you wire funds preference, provide your banking information to the title company at least 48 hours before closing. Wire transfers typically arrive within a few hours of closing — same business day.

What to Bring to Closing

  • Government-issued photo ID (driver's license or passport)
  • Your social security number (for the 1099-S)
  • All keys, garage door openers, and access codes to the property
  • Security system codes and contact information
  • Any warranties, manuals, or receipts for appliances and systems you agreed to leave
  • Proof of any repairs completed (receipts and invoices)
  • Your bank account information for wire transfer of proceeds

After Closing: Your Responsibilities

Once the deed is recorded and proceeds are disbursed, the sale is final. A few things to address in the days and weeks after closing:

  • Cancel your homeowner's insurance — don't pay for coverage on a home you no longer own
  • Transfer utilities — notify all utility providers to close your accounts as of the closing date
  • Forward your mail — set up mail forwarding with USPS immediately
  • Keep your closing documents — store them permanently. You'll need the settlement statement for your tax return and potentially for future capital gains calculations.
  • Consult your tax advisor — closing day is a taxable event. See Module 16 for an overview of what to expect and what to bring to your CPA.

Wire Fraud: The Closing Day Threat Every Seller Must Know

Real estate wire fraud is one of the fastest-growing categories of financial crime in the United States. The FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center consistently ranks it among the highest-loss crime types year over year, with billions of dollars lost annually by home sellers, buyers, and lenders. This is not a theoretical risk — it happens in ordinary residential transactions in every state, at every price point.

How It Works

The fraud follows a consistent pattern. Criminals monitor email communication between the seller, buyer, and title company — either by compromising one party's email account directly or by creating convincing spoofed email addresses that look nearly identical to legitimate ones. At a strategically timed moment, typically a few days before closing, the criminal sends an email that appears to come from the title company with "updated" wiring instructions directing your proceeds — or the buyer's funds — to a fraudulent account.

Once a wire transfer is executed, it moves within hours. Recovery is extremely rare. By the time the fraud is discovered, the funds have typically been moved through multiple accounts and are gone.

Your Protection Protocol

Follow these steps without exception, on every transaction:

  1. Never accept wiring instructions via email alone. If you receive an email with wiring instructions, treat it as potentially fraudulent until you've independently verified it — regardless of how official it looks or who it appears to come from.
  2. Call the title company at a number you verified independently. Use the phone number from the title company's official website or from your initial contact documentation — not the number printed in the email. Ask them to verbally confirm the exact wiring instructions including bank name, routing number, and account number.
  3. Be suspicious of any last-minute changes. Legitimate title companies almost never change wiring instructions at the eleventh hour. Any message claiming instructions have changed is a major red flag that warrants an immediate phone call to your established contact at the title company.
  4. Confirm outgoing wire settings with your bank in advance. Verify that your bank has your correct wire transfer limits set and that there are no fraud holds that could delay your outgoing wire if you're sending funds at closing. Do this at least three days before closing day, not on closing morning.
Treat Every Email-Only Wiring Instruction as Potentially Fraudulent

This is not alarmist — it is the single most important financial protection habit you can adopt for this transaction. Wire fraud targeting real estate closings succeeds because sellers are distracted, the transaction is emotional, and the email looks completely legitimate. The five minutes it takes to pick up the phone and verbally confirm wiring instructions is the only reliable defense. Once a wire is sent to a fraudulent account, it is nearly impossible to recover regardless of how quickly the fraud is reported. Verify by phone. Every time. No exceptions.

Common Closing Delays and How to Prevent Them

Most closing delays are predictable and, with the right preparation, preventable. The sellers who make it to closing on schedule are the ones who understand where the risks live and eliminate them in advance rather than reacting to them after they surface. Here are the five most common causes of delayed closings and the specific steps you can take to head each one off.

The Five Most Common Delay Causes

  1. Buyer's financing falls through or stalls. Prevention: Evaluate buyer pre-approval letters carefully — a pre-approval letter from a reputable direct lender carries more weight than one from an online marketplace or a broker who hasn't yet run full underwriting. If your closing extends beyond 45 days from contract acceptance, require an updated pre-approval letter from the buyer's lender confirming the loan is still on track. Watch for buyer behavior that signals financial instability: large new purchases, job changes, or requests to extend the financing contingency period without explanation.
  2. Appraisal comes in below the purchase price. Prevention: Accurate initial pricing is your best defense. If you've priced the home correctly based on recent comparable sales, the appraisal should support the contract price. If you receive a short appraisal, you have the right to request a reconsideration of value by providing the appraiser with comps they may have missed. Prepare a clean comp package in advance — three to five recently sold comparable properties with photos and adjustment notes — and make it available to the appraiser at the time of their visit.
  3. Title defects discovered during the title search. Prevention: If you're aware of any potential title issues — an unpaid contractor who filed a lien, an unresolved HOA debt, a judgment in your name, or any probate issues from a prior property transfer — address them before you list, not after you're under contract. Order a preliminary title search yourself (title companies offer these for $100–$200) to surface any issues before a buyer discovers them. A title defect found during the closing process can delay your closing by weeks or kill the deal entirely.
  4. Inspection re-negotiation collapses. Prevention: The most effective way to prevent inspection-related deal collapse is a pre-listing inspection. Hire a licensed home inspector before you list, review the report, and either repair the significant issues or price the home to reflect them. A seller who can provide buyers with a pre-listing inspection report and receipts for completed repairs is in a dramatically stronger negotiating position than one who is learning about problems for the first time during buyer's inspection negotiations.
  5. Final walkthrough reveals unexpected condition issues. Prevention: The home must be in substantially the same condition at final walkthrough as it was when the offer was made. Do not remove any fixtures included in the sale. Complete all agreed repair items with licensed contractors and have receipts ready. If you discover damage during your move-out — a wall scuffed moving furniture, a broken window shade — address it before the walkthrough rather than hoping the buyer won't notice.

Remote and Digital Closing Options

Traditional closings require all parties to appear in person at the title company or attorney's office at a specific time, sign a stack of paper documents, and hand over keys while a notary watches. That model still exists and works well — but it's increasingly optional. Remote and digital closing alternatives are now available in most markets and can meaningfully simplify the logistics of your closing day, particularly if you've already vacated the home, are traveling, or are managing multiple time-sensitive transitions simultaneously.

Remote Online Notarization (RON)

Remote Online Notarization is now available in the majority of US states and allows you to sign closing documents electronically from any location while a commissioned notary witnesses your signatures via live video call. The notary uses audio-visual technology to verify your identity through credential analysis and knowledge-based authentication questions, then applies a digital seal to the documents. RON-signed documents meet the same legal standards as in-person notarization in states that have adopted it. Your title company will tell you whether RON is available for your specific transaction and jurisdiction — not all document types can be RON-notarized everywhere, and the deed in particular may still require specific handling depending on county recording requirements.

Mail-Away Closing

A mail-away closing (also called a "signing by mail" closing) involves the title company preparing a complete closing package and sending it to you via overnight courier. You sign with a local notary at a bank, UPS store, or any location with a commissioned notary available, then return the package via overnight courier. This option is available in virtually every market and does not require any special technology. It adds one to two days to the closing timeline because the documents must physically travel both directions, but it's a completely standard option that title companies handle routinely.

Mobile Notary

A mobile notary comes to a location of your choice — your home, your workplace, a coffee shop — at a time you schedule. You sign the standard paper closing package with the notary present, and the notary returns the signed documents to the title company. Mobile notaries are particularly common in markets with a high proportion of elderly sellers, sellers who are unable to travel easily, or sellers who are in the middle of a long-distance move. The cost is typically $75–$200 for the notary's travel and service, which is a reasonable trade for avoiding a trip to a title company during an already chaotic moving period.

Hybrid Closing

Many title companies now offer hybrid closings that combine in-person and remote signing. Non-critical documents are signed electronically in advance through a secure portal, and you appear in person (or via RON) only for the handful of documents that require wet-ink or in-person notarization — typically the deed. This reduces your in-person closing time from 45–60 minutes to as little as 10–15 minutes.

Plan Remote Closings at Least One Week Out

Remote closing options — whether RON, mail-away, or mobile notary — typically add one to two business days to the closing timeline due to scheduling, courier transit, or technology setup. If you intend to use a remote closing option, notify your title company at least one week before your closing date, not 24–48 hours in advance. Last-minute remote closing requests can cause delays that ripple through your entire transaction, including the buyer's mortgage rate lock and any bridge loan or simultaneous purchase you're managing.

Understanding Your Settlement Statement

The settlement statement — formally called the ALTA Settlement Statement or the Closing Disclosure (CD) — is the complete financial accounting of your transaction. Every dollar that flows through the closing is itemized on this document. Reading it carefully before you sign is one of the most important things you can do at closing, because errors are common and catching them after the fact is significantly harder than catching them at the table.

What the Settlement Statement Covers

The ALTA/CD is organized into sections covering the buyer's side and the seller's side of the transaction. As the seller, focus on:

  • Your credits: The purchase price is your primary credit. If the buyer agreed to pay any portion of your closing costs, those appear as credits to you as well.
  • Your debits: Your mortgage payoff (the exact amount owed to your lender as of the closing date), prorated property taxes (covering the portion of the year you owned the home), any closing cost credits you offered the buyer, title company fees, transfer taxes, and recording fees.
  • Cash to seller (Section J on the ALTA form): The bottom line — your net proceeds after all debits are subtracted from all credits. This is the number that gets wired to your bank account.

The Most Common Settlement Statement Error: Prorated Property Taxes

Property tax proration is one of the most frequently miscalculated items on settlement statements, and the error can cost you hundreds or thousands of dollars depending on your annual tax bill and closing date. Here is how it works:

In most states, property taxes are paid in arrears — meaning you pay in 2026 for 2025 taxes. If you close on October 15, you owe the buyer a credit for the portion of the current year that you owned the home before the sale: January 1 through October 15, which is 9.5 months out of 12, or approximately 79% of your annual tax bill.

To verify the proration: confirm the title company used the correct annual tax amount (your most recent tax bill, not an estimate), the correct closing date, and the correct proration method for your state (some states prorate on a 365-day basis; others use a 360-day basis). A $6,000 annual tax bill prorated over 9.5 months equals a $4,750 credit to the buyer. If the title company used last year's bill and your taxes increased, you could be under-crediting the buyer — which can cause a last-minute dispute. Verify the number yourself before closing day.

Request Your Preliminary Settlement Statement 48–72 Hours Before Closing

Ask your title company to send you a preliminary settlement statement at least 48 to 72 hours before your closing date — not 24 hours, which is the minimum but rarely gives you enough time to identify and correct errors before you're sitting at the closing table. Read every line. Verify your mortgage payoff amount against the payoff statement your lender provides. Check the proration math. Confirm that any seller-paid closing cost credits match what's written in the contract. If you find a discrepancy, you have time to resolve it without holding up the entire closing. Errors caught on preliminary review take minutes to fix. Errors discovered at the closing table can take hours — and in some cases, delay closing to the next business day.

Key Takeaways

  • The closing period runs 30–45 days and involves inspection, appraisal, underwriting, and final walkthrough — each with specific timelines
  • The title company is your neutral coordinator — they hold escrow funds, search title, prepare closing documents, and disburse proceeds
  • As the seller, your primary closing documents are the deed, settlement statement, seller's affidavit, and 1099-S
  • Your net proceeds are typically wired to your bank account on closing day — provide banking info 48 hours in advance
  • Seller closing costs as FSBO are typically 1–3% vs. 5–6% for traditional listings — the difference is your savings
  • After closing: cancel insurance, transfer utilities, forward mail, and keep all documents permanently for tax purposes
  • Wire fraud targeting real estate closings is a major and growing threat — always verify wiring instructions by phone at an independently confirmed number, never by email alone
  • The five most preventable closing delays are financing issues, short appraisals, title defects, inspection breakdowns, and final walkthrough problems — each has a specific pre-emptive step
  • Remote closing options including RON, mail-away, and mobile notary are available in most markets — arrange them at least one week before closing
  • Request your preliminary settlement statement 48–72 hours before closing and verify the property tax proration math independently before you sign

Action Steps

  1. Identify the title company or real estate attorney you'll use for your closing — ask for a referral from neighbors who've sold recently, or search reviews in your area.
  2. Review a sample settlement statement (HUD-1 or ALTA form) online so you're familiar with the format before you see yours for real.
  3. Calculate your estimated net proceeds: sale price minus mortgage payoff, minus estimated closing costs, equals your check.
  4. Set up your banking information in advance — decide whether you want a wire transfer or check and notify the title company of your preference.
  5. Book a consultation with a CPA or tax advisor to understand the capital gains implications of your sale — ideally before you list, not after you close.

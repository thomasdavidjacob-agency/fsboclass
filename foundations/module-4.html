Showing Your Home Safely

Foundations Course25 min readModule 4 of 6

Showing your home to strangers is a fundamentally different experience as an FSBO seller than it is as a seller represented by an agent. When you have an agent, they handle scheduling, qualify buyers, and are typically present during showings. As an FSBO seller, you're doing all of that yourself — and that requires a specific system for safety, efficiency, and turning showings into offers. This module gives you that system.

Direct Relationships Close Deals

Every buyer who tours your home without a listing agent as the gatekeeper is a buyer you can connect with directly. Direct relationships with motivated buyers — when handled professionally — often lead to smoother negotiations and faster closes.

Qualifying Buyers Before You Show

Not every person who calls about your listing deserves a showing appointment. Many are curious neighbors, people at the very beginning of their home search with no financing in place, or even other agents trying to convert you into a client. Qualifying buyers before you let them in protects your time, your safety, and your home's security.

The Pre-Showing Qualification Questions

When a buyer or buyer's agent calls to schedule a showing, ask these questions before confirming an appointment:

  1. "Are you pre-approved for a mortgage?" Pre-approved — not just pre-qualified — means a lender has reviewed their income, assets, and credit and issued a conditional commitment. Pre-qualified means they answered a few questions online. These are not the same thing. Cash buyers should be prepared to provide proof of funds upon request.
  2. "What is your approximate price range?" If your home is listed at $450,000 and they're looking under $380,000, a showing wastes everyone's time.
  3. "What is your timeline?" A buyer who needs to move in six weeks is serious. A buyer who "is just starting to look" may not tour your home for another four months.
  4. "Are you currently working with a buyer's agent?" This matters for how you'll communicate and whether a buyer's agent commission is relevant to your situation.

You don't need to interrogate every caller. Keep it conversational. But these four questions separate motivated, qualified buyers from browsers — and your showing time is valuable.

Fair Housing Reminder

Federal Fair Housing laws prohibit discrimination based on race, color, national origin, religion, sex, familial status, or disability. You may not decline showings or apply different standards based on any of these protected characteristics. Your qualification criteria must be uniformly applied to every inquiry. Stick to financial qualification and timeline — not personal characteristics of the buyer.

Safety Protocols for FSBO Showings

Home showing safety is a topic that real estate associations train agents on extensively — and FSBO sellers often overlook entirely. Here is a complete safety framework for showing your home as an owner-seller.

1. Never Show Alone If Possible

Whenever possible, have another adult present during showings. A spouse, family member, or trusted friend provides both a safety buffer and a practical benefit — you can split up so buyers can explore without feeling watched while someone remains visible.

If you must show alone, tell someone your schedule: who is coming, at what time, and when you expect them to leave. Share the buyer's name and phone number with a trusted contact before the showing begins.

2. Verify Identity Before Showing

Ask every buyer to confirm their first and last name when they arrive. If they're coming with a buyer's agent, verify the agent's license online (every state has a license lookup tool on the real estate regulatory website). For unrepresented buyers, it's entirely reasonable to request a copy of their pre-approval letter and confirm the name matches the person at your door before the showing.

3. Secure Valuables, Medications, and Documents

Before any showing, remove or lock away:

  • Prescription medications — pill theft is the most commonly reported theft during home showings
  • Jewelry, cash, and portable electronics
  • Financial documents, tax returns, mail with account numbers
  • Keys, including spare vehicle keys
  • Firearms and ammunition — store these locked and separately
  • Personal documents: passports, social security cards, checkbooks

This is not about distrusting every buyer. It's about eliminating risk entirely so you never have to wonder.

4. Control the Showing Flow

As you show the home, position yourself strategically. Never allow buyers to go to a part of the home where they're entirely out of your line of sight for extended periods. This is particularly relevant for basements, secondary floors, and detached structures like garages and workshops.

The standard showing flow is to start at the front door, move through the main living areas, then to bedrooms and bathrooms, then downstairs or to the garage. Maintain a natural conversational presence without hovering — buyers need space to envision the home as theirs.

5. Remove Pets During Showings

Regardless of how friendly your pets are, they create complications during showings. Some buyers are allergic, some are afraid of dogs, and all buyers are distracted by an animal competing for their attention. Remove pets — ideally by taking them to a neighbor's or boarding them for active showing days.

Scheduling Systems That Work

Managing showing requests manually — via text and phone — becomes chaotic quickly in an active market. Use a showing scheduling system from day one.

Recommended Tools

  • Calendly (free): Create a booking link with your available showing times. Buyers self-schedule without back-and-forth. Set 60–90 minute windows to allow each buyer adequate time and yourself buffer between appointments.
  • ShowingTime: The industry-standard showing scheduling tool used by most agents. Your flat-fee MLS service may give you access to ShowingTime, which automatically notifies you of requests and allows one-click confirmation.
  • Text-based scheduling: For lower-volume markets, managing by text is workable — but set a dedicated showing phone number (a Google Voice number is free) so your personal number isn't widely distributed.

Setting Showing Hours

Define your showing availability upfront and stick to it. A reasonable showing schedule for most markets:

  • Weekdays: 4:00 PM – 8:00 PM (after work hours)
  • Saturdays: 10:00 AM – 6:00 PM
  • Sundays: 12:00 PM – 5:00 PM

In hot markets, being more flexible increases your offer volume. In slower markets, your showing hours matter less — qualified buyers will accommodate you. Don't make yourself available 24/7. That's not sustainable and it signals desperation to agents who know better.

Open Houses vs. Private Showings

Open houses are a topic of significant debate among real estate professionals. Here's an honest assessment of their value for FSBO sellers:

The Case For Open Houses

Open houses create a sense of social proof and urgency. When buyers see other buyers touring a home simultaneously, their perception of desirability increases. An open house also allows you to show the home to multiple parties in a single day, reducing the coordination burden of individual appointments. For FSBO sellers who are still in the home, a Saturday open house can substitute for multiple private showings.

The Case Against (Or For Limited Use)

Research consistently shows that very few homes are actually sold through open houses. Most open house attendees are curious neighbors, early-stage browsers, and unqualified lookers. Serious, pre-approved buyers typically tour homes by private appointment — often with their buyer's agent. Open houses also create more pronounced security concerns because you're allowing multiple unverified strangers into your home simultaneously.

The Best Approach

Hold one open house in the first weekend of your listing — it generates initial momentum and exposure — then shift to private showings by appointment for qualified buyers. Do not hold weekly open houses indefinitely; it signals that you're having trouble selling.

Gathering and Using Feedback

After every showing, solicit feedback. This intelligence is valuable whether you're getting offers or not.

How to Ask for Feedback

Send a text or email within 24 hours of the showing: "Thank you for visiting [address]. We'd appreciate any feedback you're willing to share — what did you like most, and was there anything that gave you pause? Your thoughts help us a great deal."

For showings conducted through a buyer's agent, you can send the same message to the agent directly. Agents are more likely to give candid feedback than buyers, who may worry about being rude.

How to Interpret Feedback

Track your feedback in a simple log. After 3–5 showings, look for patterns:

  • Repeated price concerns: The market is telling you your price needs adjustment
  • Repeated condition concerns: Identify the specific issue and address it if possible
  • Repeated positive feedback but no offers: The home is well-received but buyers may be finding better value elsewhere — revisit your pricing
  • No feedback at all: The showing went poorly enough that buyers don't want to engage — consider what the experience felt like and whether anything can be improved
The Pre-Showing Checklist

Run through this before every showing: fresh flowers or neutral scent, all lights on, temperature comfortable (68–72°F), all toilets flushed and lids closed, dishes out of the sink, beds made, personal photos away, pets removed, valuables secured, outdoor furniture arranged, driveway clear. This takes 20 minutes and meaningfully improves first impressions.

Technology Tools for the Modern FSBO Showing

The right technology stack takes your showing operation from informal to professional — and adds a layer of security and accountability that protects you throughout the process. These tools don't require a real estate license or a brokerage budget. Most cost less than $100 total to set up.

Smart Lockboxes

A smart lockbox allows buyer's agents to access your home for showings without you needing to be present — significantly expanding your showing flexibility. The two leading options for FSBO sellers are:

  • Supra iBox BT: The industry-standard lockbox used by most real estate agents. Access is managed via the Supra eKey app, which logs every access event with a timestamp and the agent's ID. Many flat-fee MLS services include Supra lockbox rental as part of their package.
  • Master Lock Vault Bluetooth: A consumer-grade smart lockbox available at major hardware retailers for around $50–$70. You control access codes via the Master Lock Vault app, set time-based access windows (e.g., showings allowed only between 9 AM and 7 PM), and receive a push notification every time the box is opened. Ideal for FSBO sellers who want access logging without the agent-network infrastructure.

Either option gives you a complete access log: who opened the box, and exactly when. This record is invaluable if anything is missing after a showing or if access is ever disputed.

Video Doorbells

A Ring or Nest video doorbell does three important things for FSBO showings: it notifies you the moment someone arrives at your door, allows you to monitor the showing remotely from your phone, and creates a timestamped video record of everyone who enters your home. Setup takes less than 30 minutes and both systems offer cloud storage for recorded footage. Even if you're present for every showing, the doorbell acts as a deterrent and a documentation tool. Store footage for at least 30 days after each showing.

NFC and QR Code Property Sheets

Place a laminated card at the front door or entry table with a QR code linking to your full property information page — square footage, lot size, year built, utility averages, HOA details, school district, and a digital copy of the property disclosure. Buyers can scan it on arrival and reference it later. This eliminates the "I can't remember the square footage" conversation that causes hesitation during follow-up.

ShowingTime Integration

Many flat-fee MLS services offer ShowingTime integration as an add-on, often for $20–$40 per month. ShowingTime is the scheduling platform most buyer's agents already use — when they search your listing in the MLS, they see a "Schedule Showing" button that routes directly to your calendar. You confirm or decline showing requests with one tap. The platform also sends automated feedback requests to buyer's agents 24 hours after each showing and compiles the responses into a dashboard. If you're in a market with high buyer's agent activity, ShowingTime integration pays for itself in hours of saved coordination time within the first two weeks.

Use a Dedicated Google Voice Number for Showing Inquiries

Set up a free Google Voice number specifically for showing-related calls and texts. This keeps your personal cell number private, creates a searchable log of every inquiry you've received, and makes it easy to review your communication history if a dispute arises later. You can forward calls to your cell so you never miss a showing request, and the web interface lets you read every text thread from any device. It takes about 10 minutes to set up at voice.google.com and costs nothing.

How to Run an Effective Open House

A well-run open house is a controlled event, not an open-door policy. The sellers who get offers from open houses are the ones who prepare systematically, manage the experience deliberately, and follow up without hesitation. Here is the complete operational guide.

Morning Preparation Checklist

Begin preparation at least two hours before your open house start time. Work through this list in order:

  • Turn on every light in the home, including closets, pantries, and the garage
  • Set the thermostat to 70°F — neither stuffy nor cold
  • Place fresh flowers (white or yellow tulips, or simple greenery) in the kitchen and main living area
  • Place your directional signs at every key intersection within a quarter-mile of your home — minimum four signs, including at the street entrance
  • Print a fresh stack of property information sheets — at least 30 copies
  • Set up your sign-in table at the entry with sign-in sheets, pens, and a stack of property sheets
  • Complete your standard pre-showing checklist: dishes, beds, toilets, pets, valuables
  • Post your open house to Zillow, Realtor.com, Facebook Marketplace, and any neighborhood apps (Nextdoor) the morning of the event

What to Have at the Sign-In Table

Your sign-in table does more work than you might expect. Set it up with intention:

  • Sign-in sheet: First name, email address, and phone number. Don't ask for last name — it creates friction. You're capturing contact information for follow-up, not running a background check at the door.
  • Property information sheet: One to two pages. Include address, list price, square footage, lot size, year built, bed/bath count, garage, basement, HOA (if applicable), average utility costs, school district, and a brief written description of the home's key features. This sheet leaves with the buyer and keeps your home in their mind after they visit five others.
  • Feature highlights card: A single, professionally formatted half-page card highlighting three to five specific selling points — the kitchen remodel, the new HVAC, the walkout basement, the school ranking. Make it scannable. Buyers read bullets, not paragraphs, when they're standing at your entry table.

During the Open House: Your Role

Greet every visitor at the door. Give a brief, two-sentence overview of the home's highlights — "The kitchen was fully renovated in 2022, and the primary suite addition was completed in 2020. Take your time looking around and let me know if you have any questions." Then step back. Let them explore without following them from room to room. Buyers need to be able to stand in your kitchen and picture their own life there — they can't do that with the seller narrating behind them.

As visitors are wrapping up and heading back toward the door, a natural conversation starter: "Do you have any questions about the home or the neighborhood?" This opens a dialogue without pressure. Be honest and specific in your answers. Buyers respect directness far more than salesmanship.

What Not to Say During an Open House

Certain statements can undermine your negotiating position significantly. Do not volunteer your motivation to sell — saying "we need to be out by June" is a negotiating gift you hand to every buyer in the room. Do not discuss price flexibility — "we have some room" is an invitation to lowball. Do not comment on other interested parties unless you have competing offers confirmed in writing — manufactured urgency that unravels during negotiation destroys trust and can kill deals. Keep conversation warm, informative, and focused on the home's features and the neighborhood.

Handling Agents Who Try to Convert You

During showings and open houses, you will encounter real estate agents. Most are there legitimately, representing qualified buyers. Some are there to pitch you on listing with them. Knowing the difference — and knowing how to respond — keeps your FSBO on track and your relationships with genuine buyer's agents productive.

The Most Common Conversion Pitches

Learn to recognize these approaches and understand the reality behind each one:

  • "I have a buyer who's interested, but they won't work with an FSBO seller." This is rarely true and almost always a fishing tactic. Buyers with an experienced agent who finds the right property will make an offer — they don't walk away from their ideal home because the seller doesn't have a listing agent. If they're genuinely interested, an offer will follow.
  • "Your home would sell much faster with professional representation." This may or may not be true, and the agent has a direct financial interest in you believing it. Ask for data: what is the average days-on-market for comparable homes in your area, and how does it compare to your current showing activity?
  • "I could get you $20,000 more for your home." Run the math before you're impressed. If they get you $20,000 more and charge a 3% listing commission on a $450,000 sale, they take $13,500. Your net gain from their "extra $20,000" is $6,500 — and that assumes their price claim is accurate, which it may not be.

How to Respond

You don't need to be defensive or dismissive. A professional, brief response closes the conversation without hostility: "We're committed to completing this sale ourselves. If your buyer is interested in the home, we'd welcome an offer through the standard process and we'll work together professionally." Then redirect the conversation to the home or the buyer's needs.

Agents who are genuinely working with buyers will move on and submit an offer. Agents who are prospecting for listings will move on and find an easier target. Either way, your response accomplishes the goal.

Buyer's Agents Are Valuable Partners

The goal here is not to be hostile to every agent who walks through your door. A buyer's agent representing a qualified, motivated buyer is one of the best things that can happen during your FSBO sale — they've already done the buyer qualification work, they understand the contract process, and they typically make the transaction run more smoothly. The distinction to draw is between genuine buyer representation and a listing pitch disguised as buyer interest. Welcome the former; professionally redirect the latter.

Documenting Your Showings: Why It Matters

A showing log is one of the least glamorous and most important tools in your FSBO operation. Start it before your first showing and maintain it through closing. You will use this record more than you expect.

What to Track

For every showing, record the following:

  • Date and time of the showing
  • Buyer's agent name, brokerage, and license number — or buyer's full name if unrepresented
  • Pre-approval status: confirmed (yes/no) and lender name if known
  • Number of buyers in the party
  • Feedback received (brief summary)
  • Follow-up action taken (feedback request sent, call scheduled, etc.)

Why the Log Matters

Your showing log serves four distinct purposes that become relevant at different points in the process:

  1. Security documentation: If anything is missing after a showing, you have a record of who was in your home and when. Smart lockbox access logs combined with your showing log create a complete chain of custody.
  2. Pricing intelligence: Twenty showings with no offers is not bad luck — it is a pricing signal. Your log makes this pattern visible. Ten showings with three requests for a second visit but still no offer is a different signal — buyers like the home but something is holding them back. Track the data and let it inform decisions.
  3. Legal documentation: If a dispute arises — whether about fair housing compliance, a showing-related incident, or a subsequent claim — your contemporaneous showing log is evidence. A log you created at the time of the showing carries far more weight than a recollection after the fact.
  4. Re-engagement list: If your deal falls through after going under contract — which happens in roughly 15% of transactions — your showing log is a list of every qualified buyer who has already seen your home. Notifying them that the home is back on the market costs you nothing and sometimes produces a signed contract within days.

Key Takeaways

  • Qualify buyers with four key questions before confirming a showing: pre-approval, price range, timeline, and agent representation
  • Never show alone if possible — have a trusted adult present and always share showing details with someone in advance
  • Secure medications, valuables, firearms, and personal documents before every showing without exception
  • Use Calendly or ShowingTime to manage scheduling and avoid the back-and-forth chaos of manual coordination
  • Hold one open house in your first weekend to generate momentum, then shift to private showings by appointment
  • Track feedback from every showing — patterns in feedback reveal pricing or condition issues before they become deal-killers
  • Smart lockboxes and video doorbells create a timestamped access record that protects you legally and logistically
  • A dedicated Google Voice number keeps your personal cell private and creates a searchable log of every showing inquiry
  • Know the three most common agent conversion pitches and how to redirect them professionally without closing the door on genuine buyer representation
  • Your showing log is a security record, a pricing tool, a legal document, and a re-engagement list — maintain it from day one

Action Steps

  1. Set up a Calendly account or Google Voice number for showing coordination before your listing goes live.
  2. Define your showing hours and include them in your listing description and any showing instructions.
  3. Create your pre-showing checklist and post it somewhere visible in your home so you can run through it quickly before each appointment.
  4. Secure a lockbox for the front door in case a buyer's agent needs to show while you're unavailable — this is optional but expands your showing flexibility.
  5. Create a simple feedback log (a notes app or spreadsheet works fine) to track themes from showing feedback.

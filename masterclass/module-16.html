Tax Implications of Your FSBO Sale

Masterclass38 min readModule 16 of 16

Selling your home has tax consequences that can mean the difference between keeping tens of thousands of dollars and writing a large check to the IRS. The tax rules governing home sales are specific, favorable in most cases, but filled with traps for the uninformed. This final Masterclass module covers capital gains exclusions, cost basis calculations, how the FSBO structure affects your tax position, 1031 exchanges, and the records you need to keep. This is education — not legal or tax advice. Consult a CPA or tax attorney for guidance specific to your situation.

Know Your Tax Position Before You Close

The $500,000 capital gains exclusion for married couples (or $250,000 for single filers) is one of the most valuable tax benefits in the tax code. Understanding your cost basis and timing could save you tens of thousands — on top of the commission you've already kept.

The Section 121 Capital Gains Exclusion

The most important tax rule for home sellers is Internal Revenue Code Section 121. This provision allows you to exclude a significant portion of your capital gain from taxable income when you sell your primary residence — potentially reducing your tax bill to zero on a substantial profit.

The Exclusion Amounts

  • Single filers: Up to $250,000 of capital gain excluded from taxable income
  • Married filing jointly: Up to $500,000 of capital gain excluded from taxable income

The Ownership and Use Tests

To qualify for the Section 121 exclusion, you must pass two tests:

  • Ownership test: You owned the home for at least 2 of the 5 years preceding the sale
  • Use test: You used the home as your primary residence for at least 2 of the 5 years preceding the sale

The 2 years do not need to be continuous or the same 2 years. If you owned the home for 5 years but rented it out for 2 years in the middle, you may still qualify if you lived in it as your primary residence for at least 2 of the 5 preceding years. The rules allow for a lot of flexibility — consult a CPA if your situation is complex.

How Often Can You Use It?

You can use the Section 121 exclusion once every 2 years. This means serial home sellers who upgrade frequently can potentially exclude significant gains repeatedly over a lifetime of homeownership — one of the most generous wealth-building provisions in the tax code.

Calculating Your Capital Gain

Your capital gain is not simply "sale price minus purchase price." It's calculated on your adjusted basis, which accounts for improvements, depreciation, and selling costs. Understanding your true gain (or lack thereof) is essential before closing.

Step 1: Determine Your Original Basis

Your original basis is typically your purchase price plus all closing costs you paid when you bought the home. This includes:

  • Purchase price
  • Title insurance you paid at purchase
  • Recording fees
  • Transfer taxes paid at purchase
  • Legal fees related to the purchase
  • Points paid on your mortgage that weren't deducted in the year paid

Your purchase price alone is not your basis. Every dollar of closing cost you paid at purchase increases your basis and reduces your eventual gain.

Step 2: Add Capital Improvements

Capital improvements made during your ownership increase your basis. A capital improvement is a permanent addition or upgrade that adds value to the home, extends its useful life, or adapts it to new uses. Common examples:

  • New roof, HVAC system, or water heater
  • Kitchen or bathroom remodel
  • Room addition or finished basement
  • New windows, doors, or siding
  • Landscaping improvements (permanent hardscape, irrigation systems)
  • Deck, patio, or pool construction
  • Electrical panel upgrade or major plumbing replacement

Routine maintenance and repairs do NOT increase your basis: painting, replacing carpet, fixing a leaky faucet, cleaning services. The test is "improvement" vs. "maintenance."

Step 3: Subtract Depreciation Taken (If Applicable)

If you ever rented out your home or claimed a home office deduction for the portion of your home used exclusively for business, you may have taken depreciation deductions on that portion. Depreciation taken reduces your basis by the same amount — meaning more of your gain is taxable. This is called "depreciation recapture" and is taxed at a maximum rate of 25%, even if your other long-term capital gains rates are lower.

Most primary-residence-only homeowners have not taken depreciation and can skip this step. If you ever rented the home or claimed a home office, consult a CPA — this calculation can be complex.

Step 4: Calculate Your Adjusted Basis

Adjusted Basis = Original Basis + Capital Improvements - Depreciation Taken

Step 5: Determine Your Net Sale Proceeds

Your taxable gain is calculated from your net sale proceeds, not the gross sale price. You can deduct from the sale price:

  • Real estate commissions paid (this is where FSBO saves on taxes too — lower selling costs = lower gain)
  • Closing costs paid by the seller at closing
  • Transfer taxes paid at closing
  • Title insurance paid at closing
  • Legal fees paid at closing
  • Cost of repairs required by the buyer's inspection (if paid by the seller)
  • Home staging costs
  • Advertising and marketing expenses (including your flat-fee MLS service, photography, signage)

Your FSBO marketing costs and flat-fee MLS fees are deductible selling expenses — they reduce your net sale proceeds and therefore reduce your taxable gain.

Step 6: Calculate Your Gain

Gain = Net Sale Proceeds - Adjusted Basis

If this number is below $250,000 (single) or $500,000 (married filing jointly), and you meet the ownership and use tests, your entire gain is excluded from taxable income under Section 121. You owe zero capital gains tax.

When You Don't Qualify for the Full Exclusion

If your gain exceeds the exclusion limit, or if you don't fully meet the ownership/use tests, you'll owe capital gains tax on some portion of the gain. The rate depends on how long you owned the home:

Long-Term Capital Gains (Owned 1+ Year)

Gain on property held more than 1 year qualifies for preferential long-term capital gains rates:

  • 0% for taxpayers in the 10% and 12% ordinary income brackets
  • 15% for most middle-income taxpayers
  • 20% for high-income taxpayers (taxable income above approximately $553,000 for married filers in 2024)

Additionally, the Net Investment Income Tax (NIIT) of 3.8% applies to gain above the Section 121 exclusion for taxpayers with modified AGI above $200,000 (single) or $250,000 (married filing jointly). This means the effective maximum federal rate on excess home sale gain is 23.8% for high earners.

Short-Term Capital Gains (Owned Less Than 1 Year)

Gain on property held one year or less is taxed as ordinary income — at your marginal tax rate, which can be as high as 37%. Avoid selling property you've owned for less than a year whenever possible from a tax perspective.

The 1031 Exchange: Deferring Gain on Investment Property

If you're selling a rental or investment property (not your primary residence), the Section 121 exclusion does not apply. However, you may qualify for a 1031 exchange — also called a "like-kind exchange" — which allows you to defer capital gains tax by rolling your proceeds into a replacement investment property.

How a 1031 Exchange Works

  1. You sell your investment property and the proceeds go directly to a qualified intermediary (QI) — a third party who holds the funds. You cannot touch the money.
  2. Within 45 days of closing, you must identify up to 3 potential replacement properties in writing.
  3. Within 180 days of closing, you must close on the replacement property using the exchange proceeds.
  4. The replacement property must be of equal or greater value than the relinquished property.
  5. The gain is deferred — not eliminated. When you eventually sell the replacement property without a 1031 exchange, the accumulated deferred gain becomes taxable.

1031 exchanges can be complex and have strict timeline requirements that cannot be extended (except in FEMA-declared disasters). Always work with a qualified intermediary and a CPA experienced in real estate exchanges. Starting the exchange process after you've already received sale proceeds (without a QI in place) disqualifies you.

State Income Taxes on Home Sale Gain

Federal tax is only part of the picture. Most states with an income tax also tax capital gains on home sales — and state exclusions, if they exist, are not always identical to the federal Section 121 rules. State tax rates on capital gains vary significantly:

  • No state income tax: Texas, Florida, Nevada, Washington, Wyoming, Alaska, South Dakota (no state capital gains on your sale)
  • High capital gains tax states: California (up to 13.3%), New York (up to 10.9%), New Jersey (up to 10.75%), Oregon (up to 9.9%)
  • Moderate states: Most remaining states tax capital gains at ordinary income rates, typically 3–6%

If you live in a high-tax state like California or New York and your gain significantly exceeds the federal exclusion, state tax can be a substantial additional liability. Factor your state's rate into your net proceeds projection.

Records You Need to Keep

The IRS requires you to maintain records to support your cost basis calculation. There is no statute of limitations on real estate records — keep them for as long as you own the property plus 3 years after the year of sale (in case of audit). Records to maintain:

  • Original purchase closing disclosure (HUD-1 or Closing Disclosure)
  • Receipts for all capital improvements — contractor invoices, building permits, material receipts
  • Any prior year tax returns claiming depreciation on the property
  • Records of any casualty losses or insurance reimbursements that affected your basis
  • Your sale closing disclosure (you'll receive this at closing)
  • All FSBO selling expenses: photography receipts, flat-fee MLS invoices, signage receipts, legal fees
The Cost Basis Record-Keeping Gap

Many long-term homeowners cannot locate their original purchase documents from 10, 15, or 20 years ago. If you've lost your original closing documents, contact the title company that handled your purchase — they retain records for many years. Your county recorder's office has the recorded deed with the purchase price. A tax professional can help reconstruct your basis from available records.

Reporting the Sale on Your Tax Return

Even if your entire gain is excluded under Section 121, you may still need to report the sale. The IRS rules:

  • If your gain is fully excluded and you did NOT receive a Form 1099-S from the title company, you generally don't need to report the sale at all
  • If you received a Form 1099-S (which title companies often issue), you must report the sale on Schedule D even if your gain is fully excluded — you show the sale and the exclusion, resulting in zero taxable gain
  • If any portion of your gain is taxable, you report it on Schedule D and Form 8949

Your title company will issue a Form 1099-S to you and to the IRS reporting the gross proceeds of the sale. This generates an automatic IRS record — do not neglect to report the sale if you receive this form.

Work With a CPA This Year

The year you sell your home is the year to use a CPA for your tax return even if you normally file yourself. A CPA experienced in real estate transactions will maximize your basis, ensure all selling expenses are captured, confirm you qualify for the Section 121 exclusion, and handle the reporting correctly. The cost ($400–$800 for tax preparation) is a fraction of the potential tax savings from optimizing your return.

Timing Your Sale for Tax Optimization

When you sell affects how much you pay in taxes, and sometimes a difference of a few weeks can save tens of thousands of dollars. Most sellers focus exclusively on the sale price and ignore the timing dimension of their tax position — which is a significant missed opportunity for sellers who have flexibility in their closing timeline.

The Two-Year Ownership Cliff

The most consequential timing decision in real estate tax planning is whether you have met the 2-year ownership requirement for the Section 121 exclusion before closing. Selling at exactly 2 years and 1 day qualifies you for the exclusion; selling at 1 year and 11 months subjects your entire gain to capital gains tax — potentially at long-term rates, but still taxable where it would otherwise be excluded. On a home with a $200,000 gain and a 15% federal capital gains rate, that two-month difference is worth $30,000 in tax savings. If you are within 6 months of your 2-year ownership anniversary, the question of whether to extend your sale timeline deserves serious consideration before you list.

Additionally, there is a second ownership threshold worth noting: the 1-year mark that separates long-term capital gains rates (0–20%) from short-term rates (up to 37%). If you've owned the home for 10 months and plan to sell in the next 2 months, extending your closing by 60 days converts a potential 37% short-term rate into a 15–20% long-term rate — a meaningful difference on any non-trivial gain amount.

Calendar Year Timing

Beyond the ownership duration question, the calendar year in which your sale closes determines which tax year the gain is reported. A contract signed in December that closes in January is a next-year tax event — the gain is recognized in the tax year of closing, not the year of contract execution. This matters in two situations:

  • You expect lower income next year: If you're retiring, transitioning jobs, or otherwise expect your ordinary income to decrease next year, your capital gains tax rate may also decrease. A gain taxed at 15% in a year where your income drops you out of the 22%+ bracket could become taxable at 0% in the following year for the portion below the 0% threshold.
  • You're near the NIIT threshold: If your modified AGI is close to $200,000 (single) or $250,000 (married), the 3.8% Net Investment Income Tax applies to your capital gain above the Section 121 exclusion. Splitting a large transaction across two tax years — or pushing it into a year where your other income is lower — can avoid triggering the NIIT entirely.
Holding Period Tax Rate on Gain Example: $150K Taxable Gain, Middle-Income Taxpayer
Less than 1 year (short-term) Up to 37% ordinary income rate $37,500–$55,500 owed to IRS
1–2 years (long-term, no exclusion yet) 15% long-term capital gains rate $22,500 owed to IRS
2+ years (qualifies for Section 121 exclusion) 0% on first $250K (single) / $500K (married) $0 owed to IRS — full gain excluded

The Home Office Deduction and Its Impact on Your Basis

If you are self-employed, run a business from home, or work remotely and have claimed a home office deduction on any prior year's federal tax return, this is critical information you must address before closing. The home office deduction interacts with your home sale in two ways that catch many FSBO sellers completely off guard — particularly the large and growing population of self-employed and remote-work homeowners who never realized this connection existed.

How the Home Office Deduction Reduces Your Basis

When you claim a home office deduction under the "regular method" (not the simplified $5/sq ft method), you are claiming depreciation on the portion of your home used for business. Depreciation is a non-cash deduction that reduces your taxable income in the year it's claimed — but it also reduces your home's cost basis by an equal amount. This is called depreciation recapture, and it becomes taxable when you sell.

Example: You own a 2,000 sq ft home purchased for $300,000. You claimed a home office deduction for a 200 sq ft dedicated office (10% of the home) for 8 years, claiming approximately $1,500 in depreciation per year ($12,000 total over 8 years). When you sell, your basis is reduced by $12,000 — meaning $12,000 of your gain is subject to depreciation recapture at a maximum 25% rate, not the potentially lower long-term capital gains rate you'd apply to the rest of your gain. On $12,000 of recaptured depreciation, that's up to $3,000 owed to the IRS that many self-employed sellers don't anticipate.

The Exclusive Use Test

The IRS home office deduction requires that the space claimed be used "regularly and exclusively" for business purposes. If the space did not meet this test in the years the deduction was claimed, the depreciation may be disallowable — but the IRS may also treat the claimed depreciation as recaptured anyway, since it was deducted on prior returns. This is a situation that requires a CPA's analysis: if you claimed home office deductions but your office was not exclusively used for business (a desk in the bedroom, a shared-use living area), the situation is complex and potentially involves both basis reduction and potential exposure on prior year returns.

Where to Find Your Depreciation History

Home office depreciation claimed under the regular method is reported on Form 8829 (Expenses for Business Use of Your Home) in your tax returns, and the depreciation amount flows through to Schedule C and Form 4562. Pull your Form 8829 from every tax year in which you claimed the home office deduction to sum the total depreciation taken. This is the number that reduces your basis.

Self-Employed FSBO Sellers: Check Your Prior Returns Now

If you have claimed a home office deduction in any year during your ownership of this home, the depreciation recapture will reduce your cost basis and may create a taxable gain you didn't anticipate — even if your total gain is well below the Section 121 exclusion limit. This affects a significant number of self-employed FSBO sellers who never made the connection between their annual home office deduction and their eventual home sale tax position. Review your Form 8829 history with your CPA before closing, not after.

Non-Residents and FIRPTA: A Brief Primer

If you are not a U.S. citizen or a lawful permanent resident (green card holder), your home sale is governed by an additional layer of federal tax law: FIRPTA, the Foreign Investment in Real Property Tax Act. FIRPTA was enacted to ensure that foreign persons who sell U.S. real estate actually pay taxes on their gains — rather than receiving their proceeds and leaving the country before filing a return. The mechanism for enforcing this is automatic withholding at closing.

How FIRPTA Withholding Works

Under FIRPTA, the buyer in any transaction involving a foreign seller is required to withhold 15% of the gross sale price — not 15% of the gain, but 15% of the total purchase price — and remit it to the IRS within 20 days of closing as a prepayment of the seller's tax obligation. On a $400,000 home sale, that is $60,000 withheld at closing. On a $600,000 sale, it is $90,000. This withholding happens regardless of whether you actually owe that much tax — the withheld amount is a prepayment that you reconcile when you file your U.S. tax return.

Reducing or Eliminating FIRPTA Withholding

There are two primary mechanisms for reducing FIRPTA withholding below the 15% default:

  • IRS Withholding Certificate (Form 8288-B): You can apply to the IRS for a withholding certificate that reduces the withholding to your actual tax liability on the transaction. This must be filed before or at the time of closing — not after. If approved before closing, the withholding is reduced to the amount shown on the certificate. If filed but not yet approved by the time of closing, the buyer must still withhold the full 15%, but the funds are held in escrow pending IRS approval rather than immediately remitted.
  • Section 121 Exclusion for Non-Residents: FIRPTA does not exempt non-residents from the Section 121 exclusion — if you meet the ownership and use tests, you can still claim the exclusion on your U.S. tax return and receive a refund of the withheld amount. However, the withholding still occurs at closing and must be reconciled through the tax filing process. You may need to file a U.S. nonresident alien tax return (Form 1040-NR) or a regular Form 1040 if you meet the substantial presence test.

Who This Affects

FIRPTA applies to sellers who are foreign nationals, regardless of how long they have lived in the United States. Common situations include:

  • H-1B, L-1, O-1, or other non-immigrant visa holders who own U.S. real estate
  • Foreign nationals who purchased U.S. property as an investment and are now selling
  • Recent immigrants who have not yet received their green card
  • Non-resident aliens who inherited U.S. real property
FIRPTA: Get Specialized Help Before Listing

If you are not a U.S. citizen or permanent resident and you own real property in the United States, consult an international tax attorney or a CPA with FIRPTA experience before you list — not at closing. The Form 8288-B withholding certificate application takes weeks to process, and the decisions you make before listing affect how much cash you actually take home at the table. The 15% default withholding on a $500,000 home is $75,000 held by the IRS until you file — an enormous cash flow impact that can be partially or fully avoided with proper planning.

Inherited Property: Special Basis Rules

If the home you are selling was inherited from a parent, spouse, sibling, or other family member, the tax rules that apply to your situation are dramatically different — and in most cases dramatically more favorable — than the rules for homes you purchased yourself. The stepped-up basis rule for inherited property is one of the most valuable provisions in the U.S. tax code for heirs, and understanding it correctly can mean the difference between owing substantial capital gains tax and owing nothing at all.

The Stepped-Up Basis Rule

When you inherit property, your tax basis in that property is generally "stepped up" to the fair market value of the property on the date of the original owner's death. The original owner's purchase price, improvement history, and accumulated gain are wiped away for purposes of your capital gains calculation. You start fresh from the date-of-death value.

Practical example: Your parent purchased the family home in 1991 for $145,000. They made $35,000 in improvements over the years, giving them an adjusted basis of $180,000. The home was worth $640,000 when your parent died in 2022. You inherit the home. Your basis is $640,000 — not $180,000. If you sell the home today for $680,000, your gain is only $40,000 (the appreciation since the date of death), not $500,000 (the appreciation from the original purchase price). On a $40,000 gain, with the Section 121 exclusion potentially available if you've lived in the home as your primary residence for 2 of the last 5 years, you may owe nothing in federal capital gains tax.

Important Caveats and Exceptions

  • Gifts vs. Inheritance: If you received the home as a gift during the original owner's lifetime (not through inheritance at death), this stepped-up basis rule does NOT apply. Gifted property carries the donor's original basis — meaning you inherit the original owner's entire gain history. The distinction between a gift and an inheritance is legally and tax-legally significant; if there is any ambiguity about whether you received the property as a gift or an inheritance, consult a CPA or estate attorney before you sell.
  • Community Property States: In community property states (Arizona, California, Idaho, Louisiana, Nevada, New Mexico, Texas, Washington, Wisconsin), both halves of community property receive a stepped-up basis when one spouse dies. This means a surviving spouse in a community property state receives a stepped-up basis on the entire property — not just the deceased spouse's half. This is significantly more favorable than the rules in common law states.
  • Estate Tax Interaction: For very large estates (above the federal estate tax exemption, currently over $13 million per person), there may be additional basis considerations related to estate taxes paid. These situations are rare for typical residential real estate but worth flagging if the estate involved significant wealth.
  • Inherited Property and Section 121: To use the Section 121 exclusion on inherited property, you must meet the ownership and use tests — you must have owned and lived in the home as your primary residence for 2 of the 5 years preceding the sale. If you inherited the home and immediately sold it without living in it, the exclusion likely does not apply, but the stepped-up basis may still result in minimal or zero taxable gain if the property was sold shortly after the date of death.

Inherited home (date-of-death value: $640K) $640,000 stepped-up basis $40,000 $6,000 (or $0 with Section 121)
Gifted home (original owner's basis: $180K) $180,000 carryover basis $500,000 $0 with Section 121 / $37,500 if gain exceeds exclusion
Purchased yourself (adjusted basis: $310K) $310,000 adjusted basis $370,000 $0 with Section 121 (married) / $18,000 excess gain (single)
Verify Your Basis Before You Sell — Not After

The stepped-up basis is not automatic in your tax records — you must establish the date-of-death fair market value through an appraisal, estate filing, or comparable market analysis at the time of death. If no appraisal was done at the time of the original owner's death, you may need to reconstruct that value from available records (MLS data, tax assessments, comparable sales). This is far easier to do 2–3 years after the fact than 15 years later. If you inherited property and no estate appraisal was performed, engage a CPA and a real estate appraiser to document the stepped-up basis before you list.

Key Takeaways

  • Section 121 allows you to exclude up to $250,000 ($500,000 married) of capital gain from taxable income — you must have owned and lived in the home as a primary residence for 2 of the last 5 years
  • Your taxable gain is net sale proceeds minus adjusted basis — not simply sale price minus purchase price; capital improvements and selling costs reduce your gain
  • FSBO selling costs (photography, flat-fee MLS, signage, legal fees) are deductible selling expenses that reduce your taxable gain
  • Timing your sale to cross the 2-year ownership threshold for the Section 121 exclusion can eliminate capital gains tax entirely on hundreds of thousands of dollars in gain
  • Self-employed sellers who have claimed home office deductions face depreciation recapture at closing — pull your Form 8829 history and review with a CPA before listing
  • Non-U.S. citizens and non-permanent residents are subject to 15% FIRPTA withholding on the gross sale price at closing — apply for a withholding certificate (Form 8288-B) before or at closing to reduce withholding to your actual tax liability
  • Inherited property receives a stepped-up basis to date-of-death fair market value — often eliminating most or all capital gains tax on the sale; gifted property does NOT receive stepped-up basis
  • Long-term capital gains (held 1+ year) are taxed at 0–20% federally; short-term gains are taxed as ordinary income at rates up to 37%
  • State income taxes on home sale gain vary dramatically — high-tax states like California (13.3%) and New York (10.9%) can add significantly to your total tax burden
  • Keep all improvement receipts and original purchase documents for the life of your ownership — basis records have no statute of limitations until the year of sale plus 3 years

Action Steps

  1. Locate your original purchase closing disclosure and all capital improvement receipts — compile them into a single folder now so your basis calculation is ready at tax time.
  2. Calculate your estimated gain using the 6-step framework in this module: original basis + improvements - depreciation = adjusted basis; net sale proceeds - adjusted basis = estimated gain.
  3. Confirm whether you meet the Section 121 ownership and use tests — if your situation is complex (rental period, partial use, recent purchase), consult a CPA before closing.
  4. If you are self-employed and have ever claimed a home office deduction, pull your Form 8829 from every applicable tax year, sum the total depreciation taken, and bring this figure to your CPA consultation before listing.
  5. If you are not a U.S. citizen or permanent resident, contact an international tax attorney or FIRPTA-experienced CPA before listing — not at closing — to determine whether you should file Form 8288-B and what your actual U.S. tax liability will be.
  6. If you inherited the property you're selling, verify that a date-of-death appraisal or estate valuation was completed. If not, engage a real estate appraiser to document the stepped-up basis value before you sell.
  7. Identify a CPA experienced in real estate transactions and schedule a consultation before you sell — understanding your tax position in advance allows you to make informed decisions about timing and pricing.
  8. Retain all FSBO selling expense receipts (photography, flat-fee MLS, signage, legal fees, home staging) — these reduce your net sale proceeds and therefore reduce your taxable gain.

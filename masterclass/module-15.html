Working With Buyer's Agents

Masterclass30 min readModule 15 of 16

More than 85% of buyers are represented by a buyer's agent. This means that as an FSBO seller, the majority of your showings, negotiations, and transactions will involve a licensed real estate agent on the other side of the table. Understanding how buyer's agents think, how they're compensated, what they want from you, and how to manage the relationship strategically is not optional for Masterclass sellers — it's essential. This module gives you a complete guide to navigating buyer's agent relationships to close more deals at better terms.

Control the Buyer-Side Commission Conversation

Buyer's agents typically receive a 2.5–3% commission — often paid by the seller. This module teaches you exactly how to navigate these conversations, what you're legally required to offer, what you're not, and how to stay in control of your proceeds.

Understanding the Buyer's Agent's Incentive Structure

To work effectively with buyer's agents, you need to understand what motivates them — and compensation is the primary driver. Buyer's agents earn a commission only when a transaction closes. They're not paid for showings, for writing offers that don't get accepted, or for time spent with buyers who ultimately don't purchase. This creates a specific set of priorities that Masterclass sellers can use to their advantage:

What Buyer's Agents Want

  • A transaction that closes: Every buyer's agent's primary goal is to get their client into a home and close the deal. They want FSBO sellers who are organized, responsive, and capable of executing the paperwork side of the transaction smoothly.
  • Clear compensation terms upfront: After the 2024 NAR settlement, buyer's agents must now have written buyer representation agreements in place before showing homes. They want to know, before they bring a client to your door, whether you're willing to cover their commission — or whether their buyer will need to pay it out of pocket.
  • A seller who won't derail the deal: Agents prefer working with sellers who understand the process, don't create unnecessary drama over minor inspection items, and respond to communications promptly. FSBO sellers with a professional approach are valued; FSBO sellers who are difficult, unresponsive, or unreasonable are avoided.
  • Showings that are easy to schedule: If your showing process is complicated, requires 48-hour notice, or involves a difficult showing experience, agents will show your home less frequently than a comparable listing with easy access.

The 2024 NAR Settlement: What Changed for FSBO Sellers

The National Association of Realtors settlement that took effect in August 2024 changed the rules around buyer's agent compensation in significant ways that directly affect FSBO sellers. You need to understand these changes clearly.

What Changed

Prior to the settlement, it was standard practice for sellers to offer a buyer's agent commission in the MLS listing — typically 2–3% of the purchase price — which the buyer's agent would receive at closing. This was essentially mandated by MLS rules in most markets. The settlement eliminated this requirement: sellers are no longer obligated to offer any buyer's agent commission in the MLS.

Additionally, buyer's agents are now required to have signed buyer representation agreements (BRAs) with their clients before showing homes. These agreements specify how the agent will be compensated, by how much, and by whom.

What Didn't Change

You can still choose to offer buyer's agent compensation — and in many markets, it remains strategically beneficial to do so. The change is that it's now truly optional and negotiable, not an MLS requirement. Sellers can offer 0%, 1%, 2%, 2.5%, or any amount they choose. They can also negotiate buyer's agent compensation as part of the offer process.

Your Strategic Options

  • Offer 2–2.5% upfront in the MLS: Maximizes buyer's agent motivation to show your home. In a competitive market where you're offering an auction-style pricing strategy, this ensures agents actively bring clients. You're saving the listing commission (2.5–3%) and paying the buyer's side — net savings of 0–1%.
  • Offer 1–1.5% in the MLS: A compromise that shows willingness to cooperate while reducing your payout. Some agents will bring buyers at this rate; others won't. Know your market.
  • Offer nothing in the MLS, negotiate per offer: This maximizes your flexibility — you negotiate buyer's agent compensation as part of each offer's terms. Some buyers will negotiate their agent's compensation into their offer price. Others will ask you to concede it as a seller concession at closing.
The Hidden Risk of Offering Nothing

Offering no buyer's agent compensation doesn't mean buyer's agents won't show your home — but it does mean some will not. Agents who have buyer representation agreements specifying their fee may steer clients away from your listing if they can't confirm you'll cover it. In a buyer's market where you need every showing you can get, this self-imposed obstacle can cost you significantly more than the commission you're avoiding.

First Contact: Setting the Right Tone

When a buyer's agent calls to schedule a showing, the interaction sets the tone for the entire relationship. Here's how to handle first contact professionally:

What to Say When Agents Call

Answer calls promptly and professionally. Don't answer with a casual "hello" — you're a businessperson managing a significant transaction. Your script:

"Thank you for calling about [address]. I'm happy to schedule a showing. What dates and times work best for your clients? I can typically accommodate showings [your available hours] with [your notice requirement] notice."

If the agent asks about commission: "I'm offering [X]% buyer's agent compensation. Is that something that works for your clients' situation?" Have your answer ready before the call comes.

What NOT to Say

  • Don't negotiate price on the phone before a showing
  • Don't over-explain why you're selling FSBO — agents will probe this; stay brief and professional
  • Don't share your timeline pressure, motivation, or minimum price with agents — this is negotiating information
  • Don't express anxiety or uncertainty about the process — project competence

During Showings With Buyer's Agents

When a buyer's agent brings clients to your home, your role is very different than during an unrepresented buyer showing. The agent is managing the buyer relationship — let them do it.

Best Practice: Leave the Home

Ideally, leave the property during agent-accompanied showings. Buyers cannot speak candidly with their agent when the seller is present, which inhibits the genuine evaluation that leads to offers. Buyers who cannot honestly discuss the home's pros and cons with their agent often pass — not because they don't like the home, but because they couldn't have the private conversation that would have moved them toward an offer.

If you must remain: stay in one area of the home (the backyard or garage are ideal) and do not follow the group room to room. Give them space.

Agents Who Bring Competing Buyers

Sometimes one agent may want to bring multiple buyer clients separately to your home. This is legitimate and common for popular listings. Schedule them as separate showings. Don't share information about other interested parties with any agent — "We have a lot of interest" is fine; "We have three other offers" before you actually have them is not.

Receiving Offers Through Buyer's Agents

Offers submitted through buyer's agents are typically prepared on standard state contract forms and submitted via DocuSign or similar electronic signature platforms. When you receive an offer:

  1. Acknowledge receipt promptly: "I've received the offer and will review it. I'll respond by [time] today/tomorrow."
  2. Review the full offer — not just the price. Terms, contingencies, earnest money amount, closing date, and any addenda all affect the offer's actual value and risk profile.
  3. If you have questions about the offer's terms, you can ask the buyer's agent for clarification — they are not your agent, but they can explain what their client is requesting.
  4. Respond with a counteroffer, acceptance, or rejection within the timeframe stated in the offer (typically 24–48 hours from submission).

Managing Agent Pressure Tactics

Some buyer's agents will use pressure tactics to move you toward their client's preferred terms. Understanding common tactics lets you respond calmly and strategically:

"This Is the Best Offer You'll Get"

This is nearly always an opinion, not a fact. The agent's job is to get the best outcome for their buyer, not to help you evaluate your market. Respond: "I appreciate you sharing that. I'm going to take some time to consider the offer and I'll be in touch." Never make a decision in the moment of an agent's verbal pressure.

"My Buyer Needs to Know by [Unreasonable Deadline]"

Buyers can always extend their offer deadline if they're genuinely interested. An agent pushing an artificially short deadline is attempting to prevent you from receiving competing offers. Respond: "I understand they'd like a quick response. I'll need [your reasonable time] to review this properly, which I'll have done by [time]."

"The Market Has Shifted, You Need to Be More Realistic"

This may or may not be true. If you've been on the market for 90 days with no offers, the agent may be right. If you listed 10 days ago and this is your first offer, treat the claim skeptically. Let data — comparable sales, active listing competition, showing frequency — guide your price decisions, not an agent's advocacy for their buyer.

"FSBO Transactions Are Complicated — You Should Work With an Agent"

This is a pitch, not advice. FSBO transactions are handled by title companies and closing attorneys who manage the administrative complexity. Your job is to negotiate terms and sign documents — tasks that are entirely within a prepared seller's capability.

Using a Real Estate Attorney

While you don't need a listing agent to handle the paperwork, having a real estate attorney review your contract before signing is a Masterclass-level best practice. A real estate attorney charge $300–$600 to review a purchase agreement, flag unfavorable terms, and advise you on contingency language. In some states (New York, Illinois, Georgia, South Carolina, and others), attorney involvement at closing is required by law. Even in states where it isn't required, the cost is trivial relative to the transaction size.

Build a Transaction Team

Before you list, identify the professionals you'll use: a real estate attorney for contract review, a title company for closing, and a home inspector if you're doing a pre-listing inspection. Having your team in place before you receive an offer means you can move quickly when you accept one — and buyer's agents appreciate sellers who can close on schedule.

Understanding and Handling Dual Agency

Dual agency — in the traditional sense — occurs when a single licensed agent or brokerage represents both the buyer and the seller in the same transaction. As an FSBO seller with no listing agent, true legal dual agency cannot occur in your transaction. However, there is a closely related situation that FSBO sellers encounter with some frequency and should handle with care.

The Conflict-of-Interest Scenario

If a buyer's agent who brings you an offer is the same agent who previously contacted you about listing your home — or who is still actively pursuing your listing business — that agent is operating with a layered conflict of interest. They are simultaneously: (1) representing a buyer whose interest is to pay as little as possible for your home, (2) trying to earn a buyer's side commission from your FSBO transaction, and (3) potentially positioning themselves to earn a listing commission on any future transaction if this deal falls through. Each of these interests points in a different direction, and none of them points in yours.

How to Identify It

Signs that a buyer's agent may have a dual-track agenda:

  • The agent called or emailed you about listing your home within the past 30–60 days
  • The agent asks detailed questions about your motivation to sell and your timeline that go beyond what's needed to write an offer
  • The agent mentions "what you could get with proper representation" while simultaneously submitting a buyer's offer
  • The agent downplays your home's value to their buyer while simultaneously telling you they could "do better" as your listing agent

How to Handle It

You don't need to refuse the offer — the offer stands on its merits. But be especially rigorous in your evaluation and consider having your real estate attorney review the contract before responding. Do not make any verbal commitments to this agent about listing with them under any circumstances. Do not share any information about your timeline, motivation, or minimum price with this agent — the conflict of interest means information you share in the context of "working together" may be used in the context of "negotiating against you."

Watch for the Dual-Track Agent

An agent who brings a below-market offer from a buyer and simultaneously encourages you to list with them "in case this doesn't work out" is not your ally. They benefit financially if your FSBO sale falls through (they get your listing) and if it doesn't (they get the buyer's commission). The only party whose interests align entirely with yours in this transaction is you — and your real estate attorney.

The Post-Offer Communication Protocol With Buyer's Agents

After you receive an offer through a buyer's agent, the communication discipline you maintain from that moment until closing determines whether the transaction proceeds smoothly or gets mired in unnecessary conflict and information leakage. Professional, prompt, and documented communication is the standard — and the failure to maintain it costs FSBO sellers money and deals with surprising frequency.

Response Time Standards

Acknowledge receipt of any offer within 2 hours of receiving it. You don't need to respond with your decision in 2 hours — but you need to confirm you've received it and set an expectation for when your formal response is coming. "Thank you — I've received the offer and will respond with our decision by [time] tomorrow" is sufficient. Full response (acceptance, counteroffer, or rejection) within 24 hours is the professional standard. Maximum 48 hours in any circumstance where you need additional time for attorney review. Offers left without acknowledgment for 12+ hours create anxiety on the buyer's side that can translate into offer withdrawal — particularly if the buyer's agent is also showing other homes.

Communication Medium: Always Email

Even if the agent calls you first, always follow up in writing. "Thank you for the call — I'll follow up via email with our formal response within [time]" creates a paper trail for every negotiation point. Why this matters: if there is ever a dispute about what was agreed, what was offered, or what was communicated, your email trail is your evidence. Phone calls are unverifiable; emails are timestamped and explicit. If an agent tells you verbally that their buyer is "willing to go to $510,000," that verbal statement is worth nothing without written confirmation — and experienced agents know this.

Information Management Rules

What to share freely:

  • The property address for the MLS status change when you go under contract
  • Your closing timeline preferences
  • Any property disclosures required by your state
  • Your attorney's contact information for contract coordination

What to never share with any buyer's agent:

  • Whether you have other offers — unless you've confirmed them in writing and are using them as negotiating leverage
  • The exact number of showings you've had
  • Your timeline pressure or deadline for selling
  • Your motivation for selling (job change, divorce, financial pressure)
  • Your minimum acceptable price
  • Any verbal commitments about price adjustments you're "considering"
Sample Post-Offer Email Sequence

Initial acknowledgment (within 2 hours): "Thank you for submitting the offer on [address]. I've received it and am reviewing it with my attorney. I'll have our formal response to you by [time] on [date]."

Formal counter (within 24 hours): "Please find our counteroffer attached. We are countering at $[X] with [specific terms]. This counter is open for acceptance until [time] on [date]. Please respond in writing to this email address."

Acceptance: "We are pleased to accept the offer as presented / as amended per our counteroffer. Please find the executed agreement attached. Our closing attorney is [name] at [contact information] — please direct your title coordination to them directly."

When an Agent Asks You to List With Them Mid-Transaction

One of the more awkward situations FSBO sellers encounter is an agent who, having shown your home to a buyer client and possibly submitted an offer, simultaneously or subsequently approaches you about listing your home with them — "just in case this deal doesn't close." This dual-track approach is not illegal, but it represents a significant conflict of interest and requires careful handling.

Why It Happens

Some agents operate strategically: they identify FSBO listings with motivated sellers, bring a buyer client (sometimes a genuine client, sometimes an investor contact), and attempt to use the offer process as a relationship entry point. If the deal closes, they earn the buyer's commission. If it doesn't, they've now established a relationship with a seller who may be ready to list conventionally after a failed FSBO attempt. The dual-track approach maximizes the agent's potential commission from your transaction in all scenarios — at the expense of your negotiating position.

How to Respond

The response is simple and non-confrontational: "Thank you — I'm committed to the FSBO approach. Let's focus on making this transaction work for your buyer." Repeat this response if the agent raises the listing topic again. Do not make any verbal commitments about what you'll do if this deal falls through. Do not allow yourself to be drawn into a conversation about commission rates, what a listed home "might get," or comparative market analyses offered by the agent as a reason to list with them.

The Listing Agreement Warning

Never sign a listing agreement with any agent while you are under contract or in active negotiation with that agent's buyer client. A listing agreement signed mid-transaction with the buyer's agent creates obvious and irreconcilable conflicts of interest — the agent is now simultaneously representing a buyer trying to pay less and a seller trying to receive more, on the same property, in the same transaction. This arrangement may not be legally enforceable and could expose both parties to liability. Any listing agreement you eventually sign — if you decide to list conventionally after a failed FSBO attempt — should be signed with an agent who has no prior financial relationship with you from this transaction.

Do Not Sign Anything Mid-Transaction

If a buyer's agent presents you with any document other than a counteroffer, purchase agreement, or standard transaction addendum — including a listing agreement, a "right of first refusal," or any form of future business agreement — do not sign it without your real estate attorney reviewing it first. Agents are licensed professionals who work with these documents daily; FSBO sellers typically do not. What seems like a routine form may create obligations that significantly affect your transaction or your future ability to sell FSBO.

Building Long-Term Relationships With Productive Agents

The transactional mindset — use each agent interaction only to close this specific deal — leaves money and opportunities on the table for FSBO sellers who plan to sell again, invest in real estate, or refer friends and family. Buyer's agents who have a positive experience with an FSBO seller become a meaningful source of future value in ways that most sellers don't anticipate.

How Agent Word-of-Mouth Affects Your Listing

Real estate agents in a given market communicate with each other constantly — at broker meetings, through MLS systems, and through informal professional networks. An FSBO seller with a reputation for being professional, responsive, and easy to work with will see that reputation generate tangible results: agents who might otherwise skip FSBO listings will prioritize yours, and agents who showed your home and had a positive experience will mention it favorably to other agents whose clients are still searching. In tight markets where agent relationships drive significant buyer traffic, your reputation as a "good FSBO seller to work with" is a competitive advantage that costs nothing to build.

Conversely

FSBO sellers with a reputation for being difficult, unresponsive, unreasonable on inspection requests, or combative in negotiations see their listings quietly deprioritized by experienced agents. Agents who have had a bad experience with an FSBO seller don't call their colleagues to warn them — they simply stop showing the listing. This invisible reduction in showing activity never shows up in any report, but it costs the seller in both time and money.

After Closing: The Professional Relationship

After a successful closing with a buyer's agent, a brief thank-you email is one of the highest-return gestures in real estate. It costs nothing, takes 3 minutes, and creates a professional memory that distinguishes you from every other FSBO seller the agent has ever worked with. Agents who close a smooth FSBO transaction remember the experience positively and actively recommend FSBO sellers they've worked with to colleagues. The thank-you email is also an appropriate time to ask if the agent has any buyer clients who might be interested in your home if anything changes — building an informal referral relationship for any future properties you might sell.

Post-Closing Thank-You Email Template

"[Agent name] — I wanted to reach out to thank you for making this transaction so smooth. Your clients were a pleasure to work with, and your professionalism throughout the process made the closing straightforward. If you ever have clients looking in this area in the future, I'd be happy to be a resource. Best of luck with your next closing."

This email takes 90 seconds to write and creates a professional relationship that may generate real value in future transactions. Send it within 3 days of closing.

Key Takeaways

  • 85%+ of buyers are represented by agents — mastering buyer's agent relationships is not optional for a successful FSBO sale
  • The 2024 NAR settlement made buyer's agent compensation fully negotiable — decide your policy before you list and communicate it consistently
  • Leave the property during agent-accompanied showings — buyers need private space with their agent to reach the genuine emotional conclusions that produce offers
  • Never share negotiating-sensitive information with buyer's agents: your timeline, motivation, minimum price, or urgency
  • Watch for dual-track agents who are simultaneously representing a buyer and pursuing your listing — evaluate their offers on merit but share no sensitive information with them
  • All post-offer communications should happen via email even if initiated by phone call — your written record is your evidence if any dispute arises
  • Never sign any document presented by a buyer's agent other than standard purchase and sale forms without your real estate attorney reviewing it first
  • Common pressure tactics (artificial deadlines, "best offer you'll get") are advocacy for the buyer — respond with calm, evidence-based reasoning, not emotional reaction
  • Engage a real estate attorney to review your purchase agreement before signing — $300–$600 for contract review is the best insurance you can buy
  • A post-closing thank-you email to the buyer's agent costs nothing and builds a professional reputation that can generate real value in future transactions

Action Steps

  1. Decide your buyer's agent compensation policy before you list — determine the amount you'll offer in the MLS and how you'll handle agents who negotiate this as part of an offer.
  2. Identify and contact a real estate attorney in your area who handles residential transactions — get their review fee, turnaround time, and confirm they're available when you go under contract.
  3. Set up a clear showing process: your availability hours, your notice requirement (recommend 1 hour minimum, 24 hours preferred), and your contact method (call/text). Put this in your MLS showing instructions.
  4. Prepare your "first contact" script for when agents call — practice it so it sounds natural. Know your commission answer before any agent asks.
  5. Set up a dedicated email address or folder for all offer-related communications — every post-offer exchange should be documented in writing, timestamped, and easy to retrieve if any dispute arises.
  6. Find your state's standard residential purchase agreement (available from your state's Association of Realtors or your title company) and read it fully before you list — you'll be signing one at some point and you should understand every section.
  7. Draft your post-closing thank-you email template now so it's ready to send within 3 days of your closing date.
